Building an Ocean in Roblox

				
A real-time FFT ocean for Roblox, a platform without vertex, pixel or compute shaders. The surface below is a live JavaScript port of the Luau implementation, constructed section by section.

				
Scroll to begin ↓

			

		


		

			

				
Chapter one · Wave synthesis

				
The sinusoid

				
We begin with a single sinusoid, plotted as a function of one spatial coordinate. Two parameters define it: the amplitude, the greatest height above the mean level, and the wavelength, the distance between successive crests. Heights in the plot are exaggerated by a factor of four for legibility.

				

				

					Mathematical detail
					
y = \htmlClass{t-amp}{A}\,\sin(\htmlClass{t-len}{k}\,x), \qquad \htmlClass{t-len}{k} = \frac{2\pi}{\htmlClass{t-len}{\lambda}}

					
A is the amplitude and λ the wavelength. The wavenumber k converts the wavelength into a spatial frequency: the phase advances by one full cycle over each distance λ along x.

				

			

		


		

			

				
Time dependence

				
Introducing time into the argument of the sine translates the profile along x without any change of shape. The Speed slider sets the rate of translation.

				

				

					Mathematical detail
					
y = \htmlClass{t-amp}{A}\,\sin\!\big(\htmlClass{t-len}{k}\,x - \htmlClass{t-speed}{\omega}\,t\big), \qquad \htmlClass{t-len}{k} = \frac{2\pi}{\htmlClass{t-len}{\lambda}}

					
ω is the angular frequency, the rate at which the phase advances in time. Crests therefore travel at the phase speed ω/k studs per second, which is the quantity the slider controls.

				

			

		


		

			

				
Superposition

				
A single sinusoid is conspicuously periodic. A sum of sinusoids with differing amplitudes, wavelengths and speeds produces a profile with the irregular appearance of a water surface. The components shown are the largest waves of the Roblox ocean this project started from, here confined to a single line: each component is drawn faintly and their sum in bold. The slider adds the components one at a time, in order of decreasing amplitude.

				

				

					Mathematical detail
					
y = \sum_{i=1}^{\htmlClass{t-count}{N}} A_i \sin(k_i x - \omega_i t + \varphi_i)

					
N components, each with its own amplitude A, wavenumber k, angular frequency ω and initial phase φ, summed point by point.

				

			

		


		

			

				
Extension to two dimensions

				
The profile is the cross-section of a surface, viewed edge-on and now drawn at its true height. Because the sum does not depend on the transverse coordinate, the surface is simply the profile extruded sideways. The lines are the edges of the triangles from which the surface is built; each vertex where they meet is a point whose position the code must compute.

				

				

					Mathematical detail
					
y(x, x_\perp, t) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} A_i \sin(k_i x - \omega_i t + \varphi_i)

					
x is the coordinate along the profile and x⊥ the coordinate across it. The sum is identical at every x⊥, so the height depends on x and t alone.

				

				

					
Platform constraint

					

						
Conventional approach

						
A vertex shader displaces every vertex on the GPU, many thousands in parallel, at negligible cost.

						
In Roblox

						
Roblox provides no vertex shaders. A Luau script must displace every vertex itself.

						
Approach taken

						
Every vertex is written from a script each frame: 18,144 of them on the top tier (the outermost ring every other frame).

						
Measurement

						
About 3.5 ms a frame in Studio with Luau's native code generation, 7.7 ms without it. Roblox documents native code generation for servers; its cost on a player's client has not yet been measured.

					

				

			

		


		

			

				
Direction of propagation

				
Ocean waves do not all travel in one direction. Assigning each component a heading causes the components to cross one another. The arrows indicate each component's direction of travel; reducing Spread to zero aligns them again.

				

				

					Mathematical detail
					
y(\mathbf{x}, t) = \sum_i A_i \sin\big(k_i\,{(\htmlClass{t-dir}{\hat{\mathbf{d}}_i}\cdot\mathbf{x})} - \omega_i t + \varphi_i\big)

					
The bold x now denotes a point on the horizontal plane, with two coordinates. d̂ is the unit vector along a component's heading, so the dot product d̂·x is the distance of the point along that heading.

				

			

		


		

			

				
Directional spreading

				
With more components the surface loses its apparent regularity, at least when viewed from near the water. The headings lie within 45° either side of one heading, approximating the angular spread of waves raised by a single wind, and the slider continues through the full set, again in order of decreasing amplitude.

				

				

					Mathematical detail
					
y(\mathbf{x}, t) = \sum_{i=1}^{\htmlClass{t-count}{N}} A_i \sin\big(k_i\,{(\hat{\mathbf{d}}_i\cdot\mathbf{x})} - \omega_i t + \varphi_i\big)

					
The same sum as in the previous section, now over N components, each with its own amplitude, heading, wavelength and initial phase. The final components contribute little: their amplitudes are small, and the very last are close to zero.

				

				

					
Platform constraint

					

						
Conventional approach

						
Each additional component costs a few shader instructions, distributed across the thousands of cores of the GPU.

						
In Roblox

						
Each additional component is a further pass of a Luau loop over every vertex, on the CPU, within the frame.

						
Approach taken

						
The component count is kept low, and this page omits further components where they would not be visible.

						
Measurement

						
While the simulation is running, the line above is measured live in this browser. In Roblox the same loop runs in Luau, on the CPU, within the same frame as all other game work.

					

				

			

		


		

			

				
Chapter two · Shading

				
Shape without shading

				
Without the wireframe the shape is no longer visible. Every point receives the same flat white, so nothing distinguishes a crest from a trough; the switch restores the lines for comparison. The appearance of water is determined less by its shape than by its interaction with light.

				

				

					Mathematical detail
					
\text{colour} = c_{\text{white}}

					
Without illumination every pixel receives the same colour, whatever the geometry of the surface.

				

			

		


		

			

				
Surface normals

				
Shading requires the orientation of the surface at every point, given by the normal vector: the arrows perpendicular to the surface here. At the centre, two further arrows lie in the surface, inclined by its slope: the tangent along x (orange) and the binormal along z (pink). The normal is perpendicular to both and is obtained as their cross product.

				

				

					Mathematical detail
					
\mathbf{T} = (1,\ \partial_x y,\ 0), \quad \mathbf{B} = (0,\ \partial_z y,\ 1), \quad \mathbf{n} = \frac{\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{T}}{\lVert\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{T}\rVert}

					
The two partial derivatives are the slopes: the change in height per stud along x and along z. T and B lie in the surface, and their cross product is normal to it. The arrows are computed from the engine's own wave sums.

				

			

		


		

			

				
Computing the slope

				
The slopes may be obtained in two ways. They may be estimated numerically, by sampling the height a short distance either side of a point and dividing the difference by the separation of the two samples: a central difference. Alternatively, since every component is a sinusoid, they may be computed exactly. The derivative of a sine is a cosine (a sine is steepest where it crosses the mean level, which is where the cosine peaks), and the slope of a sum is the sum of the slopes. Each point here carries two normals, one from the estimated slope (orange) and one from the exact slope (teal). The orange normal is drawn slightly longer so that both remain visible where they coincide; its length carries no meaning. As the sample spacing widens the two diverge, because a wider difference averages over the short waves between its samples.

				

				

				

					Mathematical detail
					
\partial_x y \approx \frac{y(x + \htmlClass{t-h}{h}) - y(x - \htmlClass{t-h}{h})}{2\htmlClass{t-h}{h}}, \qquad \partial_x y = \sum_i A_i k_i\,\hat{d}_{i,x}\cos\theta_i

					
h is the sample spacing, θ the phase of each component and d̂ₓ the x component of its heading. The central difference requires additional height evaluations at every point; the exact slope accumulates in the same loop as the height, which is how the engine computes it for these waves.

				

			

		


		

			

				
Diffuse illumination

				
The simplest illumination model makes each point as bright as its orientation toward the sun: full brightness when facing the sun directly and none when edge-on, together with a small ambient term for skylight so that regions facing away from the sun are not black. The slider rotates the sun.

				

				

					Mathematical detail
					
\text{colour} = c_{\text{sea}}\,\big(a + {\max(0,\ \mathbf{n}\cdot\htmlClass{t-sun}{\mathbf{s}})}\big)

					
n is the unit normal, s the unit vector toward the sun and a the ambient skylight. For unit vectors the dot product n·s is the cosine of the angle between them: one when facing the sun, zero edge-on and negative when facing away. The max clamps the last case, so that no face is darker than the ambient term alone. (The implementation also applies a colour and an intensity to the sun, which scale this term.)

				

			

		


		

			

				
Specular reflection and the Fresnel effect

				
Diffuse illumination alone gives the surface a matte appearance. Water is also reflective: it shows a specular highlight where the sun is reflected toward the viewer, and a reflection of the sky whose strength increases as the line of sight approaches grazing incidence. That angular dependence is the Fresnel effect, and it governs the highlight as well, since the highlight is a reflection of the sun. Each term can be disabled to isolate its contribution.

				

				

					Mathematical detail
					
\text{colour} = {(1 - \htmlClass{t-fresnel}{F})}\,c_{\text{sea}}\big(a + {\max(0,\ \mathbf{n}\cdot\htmlClass{t-sun}{\mathbf{s}})}\big) + {\htmlClass{t-fresnel}{F}\big(\htmlClass{t-spec}{(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{h})^{p}} + c_{\text{sky}}\big)}, \quad \htmlClass{t-fresnel}{F} = F_0 + (1 - F_0)(1 - \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{v})^5

					
s points toward the sun and v toward the viewer; the half vector h lies midway between them, so the highlight peaks when the sun is reflected directly toward the viewer, and the exponent p sets its width. F, Schlick's approximation to the Fresnel term, accounts for water becoming mirror-like at grazing angles: F₀ is the reflectance at normal incidence, and csky is the colour of the sky in the reflected direction. Because the highlight is also a reflection, F weights it together with the sky, and the water's own colour receives the remainder.

				

				

					
Platform constraint

					

						
Conventional approach

						
This lighting model is written directly in a pixel shader, term by term.

						
In Roblox

						
Roblox provides no pixel shaders, and Roblox staff have stated that custom shaders are not on the roadmap. Every surface is lit by Roblox's own material model: a game supplies a few images mapped onto the surface (its colour, fine-scale normals, roughness, metalness and emission), and the engine performs the shading.

						
Approach taken

						
The highlight and the sky reflection are left to Roblox's lighting, and roughness maps are painted so that foam appears matte and open water glossy. In our Studio captures, Roblox's sky reflection brightened toward the horizon, as the Fresnel term predicts.

					

				

			

		


		

			

				
Chapter three · Wave shape

				
Gerstner waves

				
Real waves have sharp crests and broad troughs, because the water moves in approximately circular orbits. Displacing each point horizontally toward the nearest crest, as well as vertically, pinches the crests into points; excessive displacement folds the surface over itself. This is the Gerstner wave, and the illustrative waves on this page have been Gerstner waves throughout, with the horizontal displacement set to zero until now. The waves of the original Roblox prototype were Gerstner waves as well.

				

				

					Mathematical detail
					
\mathbf{x}' = \mathbf{x} + \htmlClass{t-chop}{c}\sum_i \hat{\mathbf{d}}_i\,A_i\cos\theta_i, \qquad y = \sum_i A_i \sin\theta_i, \qquad \theta_i = k_i\,(\hat{\mathbf{d}}_i\cdot\mathbf{x}) - \omega_i t + \varphi_i

					
θ is the phase of each component. Each point is also displaced along each component's heading d̂, by at most the choppiness c times that component's amplitude A: the displacement is greatest midway up a slope and zero at a crest or trough. Points on both sides of a crest move toward it, so the crests sharpen and the troughs broaden.

				

			

		


		

			

				
Periodicity and tiling

				
Viewed from altitude, the construction becomes apparent. Only the few largest components are retained here, since the smaller ones would not be resolved at this distance. For this page each of the Roblox ocean's components, though not the lone sine of the first section, has been adjusted so that a whole number of its wavelengths fits across a square tile in both directions. The FFT ocean introduced below can represent only waves of this kind, and these follow the same constraint. With the spread full, as here, they fit a 256-stud tile exactly, so the entire surface is a single tile repeated: difficult to detect at deck height, conspicuous from above. The dashed lines mark the tile's edges; the waves within one square are identical to those in the next.

				

				

					Mathematical detail
					
y(\mathbf{x} + \htmlClass{t-tile}{L}\,\mathbf{e}_x) = y(\mathbf{x} + \htmlClass{t-tile}{L}\,\mathbf{e}_z) = y(\mathbf{x}) \quad\text{when every}\quad {\mathbf{k}_i = \frac{2\pi}{\htmlClass{t-tile}{L}}\,(m_i,\ n_i)}

					
ex and ez are unit steps along x and along z, and ki is the wavevector of component i: its wavenumber, directed along its heading d̂i. Waves whose wavevectors lie on this lattice all return to the same configuration after L studs. Here L is the 256-stud tile.

				

			

		


		

			

				
Chapter four · Spectral synthesis

				
Spatial and frequency domains

				
We return briefly to a one-dimensional plot. Thus far each set of waves has been represented as a curve: height as a function of position across the tile. The same waves can be represented equivalently as the amplitude present at each frequency, where the frequency of a wave is the number of times it repeats across the tile. In that representation each wave is a single spike, located at its number of repetitions and as tall as the wave. The curve is the spatial-domain representation (conventionally the time domain; the mathematics is identical) and the spikes are the frequency-domain representation. The chart converts one into the other with the same transform code that drives the ocean, the FFT, described in a later section; each component added with the slider contributes a further spike.

				

				
				

					Mathematical detail
					
y(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{\htmlClass{t-count}{N}} A_i\,{\sin(k_i x + \varphi_i)} \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \hat{y}(k)

					
Left of the arrow, the waves as a curve; right of it, the same waves as one spike each, denoted ŷ.

				

			

		


		

			

				
Fourier decomposition

				
The transform from a curve to its spectrum and back is lossless, and that is the property of interest here. A musical chord illustrates it: decomposed into its notes, with one note removed and the remainder resynthesised, it yields the chord without that note. Each tone in the chart can be switched off and on. The transform also runs in the other direction: specifying the spikes and applying the inverse transform yields the curve. The remainder of this chapter rests on that fact: the sea is described in the frequency domain and transformed into heights. The main plot continues to show the previous section's waves, summed into a single curve; the chord in the chart is a sum of the same kind, composed of audio tones.

				

				
				

					Mathematical detail
					
y(t) = \sum_{j} \htmlClass{t-note}{a_j}\,{\sin(2\pi f_j t + \varphi_j)}, \qquad \hat{y} = \mathcal{F}\{y\}, \qquad y = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\hat{y}\}

					
aj, fj and φj are the amplitude, frequency and initial phase of tone j. 𝓕 maps a signal to its frequency components and 𝓕⁻¹ maps them back; a disabled tone is a spike set to zero before the inverse transform.

				

			

		


		

			

				
The JONSWAP spectrum

				
Rather than choosing components by hand, we take their distribution from oceanographic measurement. The JONSWAP spectrum, fitted to measurements in the North Sea, gives the energy at each wave frequency for a given wind speed and fetch (the distance over which the wind has blown across open water). The JONSWAP setup follows Mihelich and Tcheblokov, and the formulas, including the directional spreading, follow Horvath.

				

				
				

					Mathematical detail
					
S(\omega) = \frac{\alpha g^2}{\omega^5}\exp\!\left[-\frac{5}{4}\left(\frac{\omega_p}{\omega}\right)^4\right]\gamma^{\,r}, \quad \omega_p = 22\left(\frac{g^2}{\htmlClass{t-wind}{U}\,\htmlClass{t-fetch}{F}}\right)^{1/3}, \quad \alpha = 0.076\left(\frac{\htmlClass{t-wind}{U}^2}{\htmlClass{t-fetch}{F}\,g}\right)^{0.22}, \quad r = \exp\!\left[-\frac{(\omega - \omega_p)^2}{2\sigma^2\omega_p^2}\right]

					
U is the wind speed, F the fetch and g the gravitational acceleration; ωp is the frequency at which the energy peaks. The peak enhancement factor γ = 3.3 sharpens the peak: the exponent r is largest at ωp and decays on either side of it over a width set by σ, so γr amplifies only the frequencies near the peak. A directional spreading function D(θ) distributes the energy of each frequency about the wind direction.

				

			

		


		

			

				
Random amplitudes and phases

				
A real sea is not a deterministic set of chosen waves. Each component drawn from the spectrum therefore receives a random amplitude, distributed about the value the spectrum prescribes, and a random initial phase; both derive from a pair of independent Gaussian random numbers, following Tessendorf (with a factor of one half in place of his one over root two, so that the variance of the field matches the spectrum). They are shown here as phasors, held stationary: each arrow is one component, its length half that component's crest height and its angle the component's initial phase. New sea draws a new realisation.

				

				
				

					Mathematical detail
					
\tilde h_0(\mathbf{k}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\htmlClass{t-seed}{(\xi_r + i\,\xi_i)}\sqrt{S(\mathbf{k})\,\Delta k_x\,\Delta k_z}

					
The ξ are independent draws from a standard normal distribution, determined by the seed (the New sea button). The random numbers come from a documented generator rather than Roblox's own, so the result is the same kind of sea as the game's, drawn from the same spectrum, but not the same waves.

				

			

		


		

			

				
Dispersion and time evolution

				
Time evolution is now enabled. The rate at which each phasor rotates depends only on the wavelength of its component, through the dispersion relation. The phasors are components of this sea, rotating at the rates the dispersion relation assigns (short waves rotate fastest, although long waves travel fastest), looping every 120 s.

				

				
				

					Mathematical detail
					
\tilde h_0(\mathbf{k}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\,{(\xi_r + i\,\xi_i)}\sqrt{S(\mathbf{k})\,\Delta k_x\,\Delta k_z}, \quad \tilde h(\mathbf{k}, t) = \tilde h_0(\mathbf{k})\,e^{-i\omega t} + \tilde h_0^{*}(-\mathbf{k})\,e^{i\omega t}, \quad \omega = \Big\lfloor \frac{\sqrt{g k \tanh(k d)}}{\omega_0} \Big\rfloor\,\omega_0, \quad \omega_0 = \frac{2\pi}{120\ \text{s}}, \quad e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta

					
d is the water depth the engine uses, 60 studs (the engine treats a stud as a metre), deep enough that tanh(kd) is close to 1 for all but the longest waves, so ω is close to √(gk) and long waves outrun short ones. ω₀ is one turn per 120 s, and the code rounds each ω down to a whole number of those (the ⌊ ⌋ brackets), so the sea repeats every 120 s. Euler's formula expresses each component as a phasor rotating in the complex plane.

				

			

		


		

			

				
The fast Fourier transform

				
Evaluating the sum over thousands of components separately at every point is prohibitively expensive. The fast Fourier transform computes the same sum for an entire grid at once by reusing intermediate results. The chart times both methods in this browser for the selected number of waves per side.

				

				
				

					Mathematical detail
					
h(\mathbf{x}, t) = \sum_{\mathbf{k}} \tilde h(\mathbf{k}, t)\,e^{i\,\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}, \qquad \underbrace{O(\htmlClass{t-n}{N}^4)}_{\text{wave by wave}} \;\to\; \underbrace{O(\htmlClass{t-n}{N}^2 \log \htmlClass{t-n}{N})}_{\text{FFT}}, \qquad \begin{aligned} X &= E + W^k O \\ Y &= E - W^k O \end{aligned}

					
For an N × N grid, direct summation costs N⁴ operations. The radix-2 FFT halves the sum recursively, and each butterfly (X and Y) produces two outputs from a single complex multiplication.

				

				

					
Platform constraint

					

						
Conventional approach

						
The FFT runs on the GPU as a compute shader, alongside the rest of the frame.

						
In Roblox

						
Roblox provides no GPU compute. The FFT is written in Luau and runs on the CPU in Parallel Luau, on one worker Actor per layer (three on the top tier), 64 × 64 per layer.

						
Approach taken

						
Every layer is kept at 64 × 64, so the whole sea is at most 3 × 64 × 64 = 12,288 wave components on a CPU script. For comparison, Acerola's GPU ocean runs about 4 million.

						
Measurement

						
One 64 × 64 inverse FFT takes 1.00 ms in Studio on the author's MacBook Air.

					

				

			

		


		

			

				
Horizontal displacement

				
Thus far the FFT displaces points only vertically. A further set of transforms over the same spectrum yields a horizontal displacement for every point, directed toward the crests, which sharpens them: the Gerstner construction, applied to the entire spectrum at once. Reducing the Choppiness slider to zero restores the rounded crests; increasing it sharpens them until the surface begins to fold.

				

				

					Mathematical detail
					
\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x}, t) = \sum_{\mathbf{k}} i\,\frac{\mathbf{k}}{\lvert\mathbf{k}\rvert}\,\tilde h(\mathbf{k}, t)\,e^{i\,\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}, \qquad \mathbf{x}' = \mathbf{x} + \htmlClass{t-chop}{c}\,\mathbf{D}

					
D is the horizontal displacement and c, the choppiness, scales it. Tessendorf writes the displacement with −i; the implementation uses +i, which moves water toward the crests, as a Gerstner wave does.

				

			

		


		

			

				
Three layers

				
A single 64 × 64 grid cannot resolve both the long swell and the short ripples, so three grids, or layers (cascades in the code), are used, tiling patches of 256, 64 and 16 studs. The two smaller layers superimpose detail on the largest, so the 256-stud pattern becomes harder to detect, although it remains present on close inspection. In this way 12,288 wave components pass for far more.

				

				

					Mathematical detail
					
h(\mathbf{x}, t) = \sum_{j=1}^{3} h_j(\mathbf{x}, t), \qquad h_j \text{ keeps only } k_j^{\min} \le \lvert\mathbf{k}\rvert < k_j^{\max}, \qquad \htmlClass{t-layer}{L_j} = 256,\ 64,\ 16

					
Each layer retains only the wavelengths suited to the size of its patch, so no component is counted twice.

				

				

					
Platform constraint

					

						
Conventional approach

						
Each layer is a GPU pass, and all of them complete within every frame.

						
In Roblox

						
The FFT runs on one worker Actor per layer (three on the top tier), and the layers are computed in turn: each layer received a fresh result only every third frame.

						
Approach taken

						
Each layer's previous result is cross-faded to its new one over an interval counted in frames.

						
Measurement

						
Before the cross-fade, the motion looked like 20 fps: the game ran at about 44 fps, but the waves updated only 15 times a second.

					

				

			

		


		

			

				
Chapter five · Surface representation

				
Output fields

				
The FFT does not return a surface but grids of values, one per cell, analogous to the pixels of an image. In graphics such a grid is called a texture, and each of its cells a texel. A texture may hold colours, but these hold heights, slopes and horizontal displacements, one set per layer. Three of them are shown for the largest layer, computed live in this browser and rendered as images: blue below zero, pale at zero, amber above.

				

				
				

					Mathematical detail
					
h_{ij},\ \ \partial_x h_{ij},\ \ D_{x,ij}, \quad i, j = 0 \ldots N-1

					
The height h, the slope along x and the horizontal displacement along x, one value per cell: column i, row j of an N × N grid.

				

			

		


		

			

				
Bilinear sampling

				
Reading a value from a texture is called sampling. A mesh vertex (the vertices are shown in the next section) need not coincide with a grid point; where it does not, its value is interpolated from the four surrounding grid values, weighted by proximity. This is bilinear sampling. The FFT grid is also periodic: its last column continues into its first. A point beyond the last column therefore wraps around and interpolates with the first, so the surface tiles without a seam. The marker oscillates across that boundary, which is drawn as a dashed line.

				

				
				

					Mathematical detail
					
h(u, v) = {(1-f_u)(1-f_v)\,h_{00}} + {f_u(1-f_v)\,h_{10}} + {(1-f_u)f_v\,h_{01}} + {f_u f_v\,h_{11}}, \quad \text{indices} \bmod N

					
fu and fv are the fractional position of the point between its grid neighbours; the indices wrap at the boundary of the grid.

				

				

					
Platform constraint

					

						
Conventional approach

						
The texture-filtering hardware of the GPU performs this interpolation at no cost, for every vertex and every pixel, wrap-around addressing included.

						
In Roblox

						
Roblox's renderer samples the images supplied to it (described in the section on surface maps), but without shader access nothing samples the height grids for the vertices of the mesh.

						
Approach taken

						
The interpolation is written in Luau and evaluated for every vertex not covered by a finer ring, whether on screen or not, for each layer that the vertex's ring uses, every frame (the outermost ring every other frame).

					

				

			

		


		

			

				
Mesh structure

				
The sampled values are applied to a mesh: concentric rings of vertices centred on the point the camera views, each ring with twice the vertex spacing of the ring inside it, so that resolution is concentrated where the viewer is looking. The rings snap to a fixed world grid as the camera moves, which prevents the waves from swimming (appearing to slide as the vertices move beneath them). Here the sea is calmed and viewed from above so that the rings are visible: nested squares, finest at the centre.

				

				

					Mathematical detail
					
\Delta_r = 2^r\ \text{studs}, \qquad \mathbf{c}_r = 2\Delta_r\,\operatorname{round}\big(\mathbf{p} / 2\Delta_r\big)

					
Ring r has its vertices Δr studs apart, and its centre snaps to the point of the lattice of spacing twice Δr nearest to p, the point the camera views. The factor of two ensures that the boundary of each ring falls on vertices of the next, so that adjacent rings continue to meet.

				

				

					
Platform constraint

					

						
Conventional approach

						
A mesh is uploaded to the GPU once, and tessellation hardware adds triangles near the camera and removes them at a distance, dynamically.

						
In Roblox

						
Roblox provides no means of supplying a custom deforming mesh to the GPU, and no tessellation. The surface must be built by a script at run time with EditableMesh, and any detail it has must already be present in the mesh.

						
Approach taken

						
Rings of patches around the camera, each ring twice as coarse as the one inside it, snapped to a fixed world grid as the camera moves so that nothing swims, together with a horizon that extends to the edge of the world.

						
Measurement

						
224 patches in 5 rings on the top tier, with vertex spacings of 2, 4, 8, 16 and 32 studs, ring by ring.

					

				

			

		


		

			

				
Surface maps

				
The grids described so far displace the mesh. The appearance derives from a second kind of texture: images that Roblox maps onto the mesh and samples itself, per pixel, on the GPU, with an interpolation much like that of the section above. Three are shown here: the colour map, an emission mask (the strength of the glow at each point) and the normal map, which stores a direction in every texel so that the lighting responds to detail far finer than the triangles of the mesh. Roblox does not allow these to be computed per pixel, so two worker threads paint them into images and upload them a portion at a time. This page does the same; the maps shown are its own, live in this browser, as its painters produce them.

				

				
				

					Mathematical detail
					
\text{normal rgb} = \tfrac{1}{2}(n_x,\ n_z,\ n_y) + \tfrac{1}{2}

					
Each texel of the normal map encodes the surface normal as a colour: its x, z and y components, each remapped from the signed unit interval to the range a texel can store. (z precedes y because a normal map stores the vertical direction in its third, blue, channel.)

				

				

					
Platform constraint

					

						
Conventional approach

						
A pixel shader computes the colour of every pixel on screen, every frame.

						
In Roblox

						
Roblox provides no pixel shaders. Colour must be painted into an image and mapped onto the mesh, and fine detail must come from a normal map. A game script also cannot replace the images of a material (only plugins can): every image must be supplied when the material is created, after which only its pixels can be rewritten. That was also the only way the normal map functioned on a script-built mesh; assigning it afterwards, as a plugin can, broke the specular response.

						
Approach taken

						
The colour map, emission mask and normal map are painted on two worker threads, and each frame a portion is uploaded to Roblox: one band of the colour map, and alternately the emission mask or the normal map.

						
Measurement

						
Measured before foam was added, when one whole image was uploaded per frame: 0.037 ms of script time in Studio. Each frame now uploads a band of the colour map and either the emission mask or the normal map (and, once per cycle, the roughness maps of the foam); that cost has not been measured separately.

					

				

			

		


		

			

				
Chapter six · Appearance

				
Foam

				
Where a wave steepens enough to fold, it breaks, and breaking water appears white. The Jacobian determinant of the horizontal displacement identifies exactly where the surface is compressed; each frame foam is added there and existing foam decays slightly, so that it trails off in streaks.

				

				

					Mathematical detail
					
J = {(1 + c\,\partial_x D_x)(1 + c\,\partial_z D_z)} - {(c\,\partial_x D_z)^2}, \qquad f \leftarrow \operatorname{clamp}\!\big(\htmlClass{t-fade}{\delta}\,f + \beta\,{\max(0,\ \htmlClass{t-whitecap}{w} - J)},\ 0,\ 1\big)

					
J, the Jacobian determinant of the horizontal mapping from x to x + cD, measures how strongly the horizontal displacement (c times D, from the section on horizontal displacement) compresses the surface, and falls below the whitecap threshold w where the surface folds. (∂xDz and ∂zDx are equal, hence the square.) f is the foam coverage: it grows by β times the amount by which J falls below w, and everywhere it retains a fraction δ of itself each step.

				

				

					
Platform constraint

					

						
Conventional approach

						
Foam is held in a texture that the GPU updates in place, every frame.

						
In Roblox

						
Without pixel shaders, foam must be painted into the colour map along with everything else.

						
Approach taken

						
The foam is simulated on a painter worker and painted into the colour map one quarter at a time.

						
Measurement

						
A 256 × 256 foam field, painted into the 512 × 512 colour map.

					

				

			

		


		

			

				
Scattered light

				
The final term is the effect for which Sea of Thieves is known: wave crests glow blue-green when the sun shines through them. It approximates light scattered within the water, and is strongest near the crests when the viewer faces the sun. The term is adapted from the first scattering term of Mihelich and Tcheblokov, with different exponents and a height mask of its own.

				

				

					Mathematical detail
					
E = \htmlClass{t-glow}{\kappa}\,\max(0,\ -\mathbf{s}\cdot\mathbf{v})^{3}\,\big(\tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{2}\,\htmlClass{t-sun}{s_y}\big), \qquad \text{glow} = E \cdot M(h)

					
κ is the glow strength. v points from the water toward the viewer and s toward the sun, so E is largest when looking toward a low sun. M(h) is a mask derived from the height of the wave above the mean surface: crests glow and troughs do not.

				

				

					
Platform constraint

					

						
Conventional approach

						
A lighting hook or a pixel shader adds the scattered light, pixel by pixel.

						
In Roblox

						
Roblox provides no lighting hooks. The only relevant quantity a script can vary is the emission strength of each material.

						
Approach taken

						
An emission mask painted from the waves, combined with one glow strength per patch, set on 232 separate materials on the top tier (224 patches plus 8 horizon pieces) every frame. The mask published for Sea of Thieves performed poorly on these waves when measured (it lit their flanks rather than their crests), so the mask is derived instead from height above the mean surface.

						
Measurement

						
The 232 glow writes take 0.062 ms a frame in Studio.

					

				

			

		


		

			

				
The complete system

				
All components together: layers of FFT waves computed on the CPU, foam, scattered light and painted surface maps, with a script displacing every vertex. On the top tier this amounts to three layers, 232 materials and 18,144 vertices. Every preceding section remains interactive.

Summary of platform constraints

				
    
					
  • No vertex shaders: a script displaces every vertex, every frame (the outermost ring every other frame).
    • 
					
  • No GPU parallelism for the wave sum: each additional component is another pass of a Luau loop over every vertex, so the count is kept low.
    • 
					
  • No pixel shaders for lighting: Roblox's own material model provides the highlight and the sky reflection.
    • 
					
  • No GPU compute: the FFT runs in Luau on one worker Actor per layer.
    • 
					
  • Layers are computed in turn: a cross-fade counted in frames conceals the staggered updates.
    • 
					
  • No texture sampling from scripts: the heights are interpolated in Luau, vertex by vertex.
    • 
					
  • No custom deforming meshes on the GPU and no tessellation: rings of patches are built with EditableMesh.
    • 
					
  • No pixel shaders: colour, emission and normals are painted into images.
    • 
					
  • No texture replacement from game scripts: every image is supplied when the material is created.
    • 
					
  • No pixel shaders for foam: it is painted into the colour map, one quarter at a time.
    • 
					
  • No lighting hooks: 232 materials on the top tier, each with its own emission strength.
    • 
				

			


			

				
Assumptions refuted by testing

				
Each of the following was treated as fact until a test showed otherwise.

				
    
					
  • EditableMesh is capped at 8 meshes per player. It is a shared memory budget; fixed-size meshes are charged for the memory they actually use.
    • 
					
  • A script can assign an editable image to a material by setting its ColorMapContent. Only plugins can; a game script must use CreateSurfaceAppearanceAsync.
    • 
					
  • Normal maps do not work on script-built meshes. They do, provided the map is supplied when the material is created.
    • 
					
  • Luau costs about 0.1 microseconds per arithmetic operation. The measured cost is about 0.0026 microseconds, roughly 40 times faster.
    • 
					
  • Texture coordinates remain precise far from the origin. At texture coordinates of 100,000 the texels tore apart, so the surface re-bases its coordinates by whole tiles.
    • 
					
  • Normal maps are visible at any distance. Visibility depends on the graphics quality level: at level 6 the ripples are gone by 170 studs.
    • 
					
  • A running Studio session loads a newly installed plugin. It does not; Studio must be restarted.
