Building an Ocean in Roblox

				
I built this in Roblox, and Roblox fought me the whole way. The sea on this page is a port of that ocean, running live in your browser.

				
Scroll to build it from nothing ↓

			

		


		

			

				
Chapter one · One wave

				
One sine wave

				
Let's start with no ocean at all, just a graph. This is a sine wave: a height that rises and falls as you move along. Two knobs describe it: how tall it gets, and how far it is from one crest to the next. I've drawn the heights four times taller than they really are so you can see them.

				

				

					The math
					
y = \htmlClass{t-amp}{A}\,\sin(\htmlClass{t-len}{k}\,x), \qquad \htmlClass{t-len}{k} = \frac{2\pi}{\htmlClass{t-len}{\lambda}}

					
A is the height and λ the length from crest to crest. k, the wavenumber, turns that length into how quickly the sine cycles as you move along x: once every λ studs.

				

			

		


		

			

				
Make it move

				
Now let the clock run. At every moment the curve is drawn a little further along, so the whole wave slides sideways without changing shape. The Speed slider sets how fast.

				

				

					The math
					
y = \htmlClass{t-amp}{A}\,\sin\!\big(\htmlClass{t-len}{k}\,x - \htmlClass{t-speed}{\omega}\,t\big), \qquad \htmlClass{t-len}{k} = \frac{2\pi}{\htmlClass{t-len}{\lambda}}

					
ω is how quickly the wave cycles in time, so the crests travel at ω/k studs a second. That speed is what the slider sets.

				

			

		


		

			

				
Adding waves together

				
One wave looks like a machine. Add a few with different heights, lengths and speeds and the sum starts to wander like water. These are the tallest waves of the Roblox ocean I started from, laid along one line for now: each one faint, their sum in bold. The slider adds them one at a time, tallest first.

				

				

					The math
					
y = \sum_{i=1}^{\htmlClass{t-count}{N}} A_i \sin(k_i x - \omega_i t + \varphi_i)

					
N waves, each with its own height A, wavenumber k, speed ω and starting phase φ, added up point by point.

				

			

		


		

			

				
From a line to a surface

				
Here's the trick: that curve was never just a line. It's the edge of a surface, seen from the side. Nothing in the sum depends on the other direction, so the curve is just stretched sideways into a sheet. The lines on it are the triangles the surface is built from, and every corner where they meet is a point the code has to move.

				

				

					The math
					
y(x, x_\perp, t) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} A_i \sin(k_i x - \omega_i t + \varphi_i)

					
x is how far along the line you are, and x⊥ how far across it. The same sum at every x⊥: the height depends on x and time only.

				

				

					
Roblox says no

					

						
Normally

						
A vertex shader moves every point on the GPU, thousands at a time, almost for free.

						
In Roblox

						
No vertex shaders. A Luau script has to move every point itself.

						
What I did

						
Wrote every point from a script, every frame: 18,144 of them on the top tier (the outermost ring every other frame).

						
The number

						
About 3.5 ms a frame in Studio with Luau's native code generation, 7.7 ms without it. Roblox documents native code for servers, and I haven't measured a player's client yet.

					

				

			

		


		

			

				
Waves going somewhere

				
Real waves don't all march the same way. Give each one a heading and they start crossing each other. The arrows show where each wave is going; drag Spread down to zero and they all line up again.

				

				

					The math
					
y(\mathbf{x}, t) = \sum_i A_i \sin\big(k_i\,{(\htmlClass{t-dir}{\hat{\mathbf{d}}_i}\cdot\mathbf{x})} - \omega_i t + \varphi_i\big)

					
The bold x is now a point on the sea, with two coordinates. d̂ is an arrow of length one along a wave's heading, so d̂·x (a dot product) is how far along that heading the point sits.

				

			

		


		

			

				
Lots of waves, lots of directions

				
Add more of them and it stops looking regular, at least from down here. Their headings fan out within 45° either side of one heading, the way waves raised by one wind spread out, and this slider goes on through the whole bank, still adding them tallest first.

				

				

					The math
					
y(\mathbf{x}, t) = \sum_{i=1}^{\htmlClass{t-count}{N}} A_i \sin\big(k_i\,{(\hat{\mathbf{d}}_i\cdot\mathbf{x})} - \omega_i t + \varphi_i\big)

					
The same sum as the last step, now with N waves, each with its own height, heading, length and starting phase. The last few add little or nothing: they're tiny, and the very last have almost no height at all.

				

				

					
Roblox says no

					

						
Normally

						
Each extra wave is a few more instructions in a shader, spread across the GPU's thousands of cores.

						
In Roblox

						
Each extra wave is another trip of a Luau loop over every point, on the CPU, inside the frame.

						
What I did

						
Kept the count down, and the page keeps fewer still where the small ones wouldn't show.

						
The number

						
When the ocean is running, the line above is measured live in your browser. In Roblox the same loop runs in Luau, on the CPU, in the same frame as everything else.

					

				

			

		


		

			

				
Chapter two · Light

				
Shape isn't enough

				
Take the wireframe away and the shape vanishes. Every point is the same flat white, so nothing tells a crest from a trough; flip the switch to bring the lines back and compare. Shape isn't what makes water look like water. Light is.

				

				

					The math
					
\text{colour} = c_{\text{white}}

					
Without light every pixel gets the same colour, whatever the surface is doing.

				

			

		


		

			

				
Which way the surface faces

				
To light a surface you need to know which way each bit of it faces. That direction is called the normal: the arrows sticking straight out of the surface here. At the centre, two more arrows lie along the surface, tipped by its slope: one pointing along x (the tangent) and one along z (the binormal). The arrow at right angles to both of them is the normal; the math calls that a cross product.

				

				

					The math
					
\mathbf{T} = (1,\ \partial_x y,\ 0), \quad \mathbf{B} = (0,\ \partial_z y,\ 1), \quad \mathbf{n} = \frac{\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{T}}{\lVert\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{T}\rVert}

					
The two ∂ terms are the slopes: how much the height changes per stud along x, and along z. T and B lie in the surface, and their cross product points straight out of it. These arrows come from the engine's own wave sums, not from a picture.

				

			

		


		

			

				
Getting the slope

				
So where do the slopes come from? You can measure them: read the height a little way either side of a point and divide the difference by the distance between the two. That's a central difference. Or, since every wave is a sine, work the slope out exactly: a sine's slope is a cosine (a sine is steepest where it crosses the middle, which is just where a cosine peaks), and adding waves adds their slopes. Each point here gets two normals, one from the measured slope (orange) and one from the exact slope (teal). The orange one is drawn a little longer so both colours show where they overlap; its extra length means nothing. Widen the spacing and watch them drift apart: the further apart the two samples, the more the measurement smooths over the small waves between them.

				

				

				

					The math
					
\partial_x y \approx \frac{y(x + \htmlClass{t-h}{h}) - y(x - \htmlClass{t-h}{h})}{2\htmlClass{t-h}{h}}, \qquad \partial_x y = \sum_i A_i k_i\,\hat{d}_{i,x}\cos\theta_i

					
h is the sample spacing, θ each wave's angle inside its sine, and d̂ₓ the x part of its heading. The measured slope needs extra heights at every point; the exact one falls out of the same loop that adds the waves up, which is what the engine does for these waves.

				

			

		


		

			

				
Sunlight

				
Now light. The simplest rule: a point is as bright as it faces the sun. Facing it head on, full brightness; edge on, none; plus a little light from the sky everywhere so the shadows aren't black. Swing the sun round with the slider.

				

				

					The math
					
\text{colour} = c_{\text{sea}}\,\big(a + {\max(0,\ \mathbf{n}\cdot\htmlClass{t-sun}{\mathbf{s}})}\big)

					
n is the normal, s points at the sun and a is the soft light from the sky. For two arrows of length one, n·s (a dot product) is the cosine of the angle between them: one facing the sun, zero edge on, below zero facing away; the max keeps those faces from going darker than the sky alone. (The code also tints and brightens the sun, which only scales that term.)

				

			

		


		

			

				
Highlights, Fresnel and the sky

				
Sunlight alone makes the sea look matte, like painted clay. Water also reflects: a hot highlight where the sun bounces straight at you, and the sky itself, mirrored more and more as you look across the surface instead of down into it. That 'more and more' is Fresnel, and the highlight follows it too, since it's a reflection of the sun. Switch each one off to see what it was doing.

				

				

					The math
					
\text{colour} = {(1 - \htmlClass{t-fresnel}{F})}\,c_{\text{sea}}\big(a + {\max(0,\ \mathbf{n}\cdot\htmlClass{t-sun}{\mathbf{s}})}\big) + {\htmlClass{t-fresnel}{F}\big(\htmlClass{t-spec}{(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{h})^{p}} + c_{\text{sky}}\big)}, \quad \htmlClass{t-fresnel}{F} = F_0 + (1 - F_0)(1 - \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{v})^5

					
s points at the sun and v at your eye; h is halfway between them, so the highlight peaks when the sun reflects straight at you, and the power p sets how tight it is. F (Schlick's Fresnel term) is why water turns into a mirror when you look across it: F₀ is the little it reflects looking straight down, and csky is the sky's colour in the reflected direction. The highlight is a reflection too, so F weighs it along with the sky, and the sea's own colour gets what is left.

				

				

					
Roblox says no

					

						
Normally

						
You write this lighting yourself, in a pixel shader, one term at a time.

						
In Roblox

						
No pixel shaders, and Roblox staff have said custom shaders aren't on the roadmap. It lights every surface with its own material model; a game hands it a few images to wrap the surface in (its colour, the fine bumps, how rough and how metallic it is, and where it glows), and Roblox does the rest.

						
What I did

						
Let Roblox do the highlight and the sky reflection, and painted roughness maps so foam looks dull and open water shines. In my Studio captures, Roblox's sky reflection got brighter toward the horizon, the way Fresnel says it should.

					

				

			

		


		

			

				
Chapter three · Better waves

				
Sharper peaks

				
Real waves have sharp tops and wide troughs, because the water moves in circles. Slide each point toward the nearest crest as well as up and the tops pinch into points; push too far and they fold over themselves. That's a Gerstner wave, and it's what this page's teaching waves have been all along, with the sideways slide set to zero until now. The original Roblox prototype's waves were Gerstner waves too.

				

				

					The math
					
\mathbf{x}' = \mathbf{x} + \htmlClass{t-chop}{c}\sum_i \hat{\mathbf{d}}_i\,A_i\cos\theta_i, \qquad y = \sum_i A_i \sin\theta_i, \qquad \theta_i = k_i\,(\hat{\mathbf{d}}_i\cdot\mathbf{x}) - \omega_i t + \varphi_i

					
θ is each wave's angle inside its sine. Each point also slides along each wave's heading d̂, by up to the choppiness c times that wave's height A: the most halfway up a slope, none on a crest or in a trough. Points on both sides of a crest slide toward it, so the tops bunch up and the troughs stretch out.

				

			

		


		

			

				
The tiling problem

				
Fly up and the trick falls apart. The page keeps only the 4 tallest (the small ones would blur at this distance). For this page I nudged each of the Roblox ocean's waves, but not the lone sine from the start, so a whole number of its crests fits across a square tile, both ways. The FFT ocean coming up can only make waves like that, so these follow its rule. With the spread full, as it is here, they fit a 256-stud tile exactly, so the whole sea is one tile copied over and over: hard to spot from the deck, wallpaper from up here.

				

				

					The math
					
y(\mathbf{x} + \htmlClass{t-tile}{L}\,\mathbf{e}_x) = y(\mathbf{x} + \htmlClass{t-tile}{L}\,\mathbf{e}_z) = y(\mathbf{x}) \quad\text{when every}\quad {\mathbf{k}_i = \frac{2\pi}{\htmlClass{t-tile}{L}}\,(m_i,\ n_i)}

					
ex and ez are steps of one stud along x and along z, and the bold ki is wave i's wavevector: its k, pointed along its heading d̂i. Waves whose wavevectors sit on that grid all line up again after L studs. Here L is the 256-stud tile.

				

			

		


		

			

				
Chapter four · The real ocean

				
Time and frequency

				
Back to a flat graph for a minute. So far each wave has been a curve: its height along the tile. The same waves can be written another way, as a list of how much of each frequency there is, where a wave's frequency is how many times it repeats across the tile. In that list each wave is a single spike, placed at its number of repeats and as tall as the wave. That's the title: the curve is the time side (here it runs along distance, but the math is the same) and the spikes the frequency side. The chart below turns one into the other with the same transform code the ocean runs on, the FFT, which gets its own step soon; add waves with the slider and a spike appears for each.

				

				
				

					The math
					
y(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{\htmlClass{t-count}{N}} A_i\,{\sin(k_i x + \varphi_i)} \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \hat{y}(k)

					
On the left of the arrow, the waves as a curve; on the right, the same waves as one spike each, written ŷ.

				

			

		


		

			

				
Taking a chord apart

				
Going from a curve to its spikes and back loses nothing, and that's the useful part. Think of a chord: split it into its notes, drop one, put it back together, and you get the chord without that note. Switch the tones off and on. It works the other way too: write down the spikes you want, run the transform backwards, and out comes the curve. That's the trick the rest of this chapter uses: describe the sea as spikes, then turn them into heights.

				

				
				

					The math
					
y(t) = \sum_{j} \htmlClass{t-note}{a_j}\,{\sin(2\pi f_j t + \varphi_j)}, \qquad \hat{y} = \mathcal{F}\{y\}, \qquad y = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\hat{y}\}

					
aj, fj and φj are each tone's loudness, pitch and starting point. 𝓕 turns a signal into its frequencies and 𝓕⁻¹ turns them back; a tone switched off is a spike set to zero before going back.

				

			

		


		

			

				
Real ocean data

				
Instead of picking waves by hand, ask oceanographers. The JONSWAP spectrum, fitted to North Sea measurements, gives the energy at each wave frequency for a wind speed and a fetch (how far the wind has blown over open water).

				

				
				

					The math
					
S(\omega) = \frac{\alpha g^2}{\omega^5}\exp\!\left[-\frac{5}{4}\left(\frac{\omega_p}{\omega}\right)^4\right]\gamma^{\,r}, \quad \omega_p = 22\left(\frac{g^2}{\htmlClass{t-wind}{U}\,\htmlClass{t-fetch}{F}}\right)^{1/3}, \quad \alpha = 0.076\left(\frac{\htmlClass{t-wind}{U}^2}{\htmlClass{t-fetch}{F}\,g}\right)^{0.22}, \quad r = \exp\!\left[-\frac{(\omega - \omega_p)^2}{2\sigma^2\omega_p^2}\right]

					
U is the wind speed, F the fetch and g gravity; ωp is where the energy peaks. γ = 3.3 sharpens the peak: r, the peak-shape exponent, is largest at ωp and dies away on either side of it over a width set by σ, so γr lifts only the waves near the peak. A spreading function D(θ) fans each wave's energy out around the wind direction.

				

			

		


		

			

				
A random ocean

				
A real sea isn't a neat set of chosen waves. So each wave from the spectrum gets a random height, scattered around the size the spectrum asks for, and a random starting angle; both come from a pair of random numbers drawn from a bell curve. Here they are as arrows, held still: each arrow is one wave, its length half that wave's crest height and its angle where the wave starts. Press New sea for a different roll of the dice.

				

				
				

					The math
					
\tilde h_0(\mathbf{k}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\htmlClass{t-seed}{(\xi_r + i\,\xi_i)}\sqrt{S(\mathbf{k})\,\Delta k_x\,\Delta k_z}

					
The ξ are random numbers from a bell curve, picked by the seed (the New sea button). The random numbers come from a documented generator rather than Roblox's own, so this is the same kind of sea as the game's, not the same waves.

				

			

		


		

			

				
Setting it moving

				
Now let the clock run. How fast each arrow turns depends only on its wave's length, a rule called the dispersion. The arrows are real waves from this sea, turning at the speeds the dispersion gives them (short waves turn fastest, though long waves travel fastest), looping every 120 s.

				

				
				

					The math
					
\tilde h_0(\mathbf{k}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\,{(\xi_r + i\,\xi_i)}\sqrt{S(\mathbf{k})\,\Delta k_x\,\Delta k_z}, \quad \tilde h(\mathbf{k}, t) = \tilde h_0(\mathbf{k})\,e^{-i\omega t} + \tilde h_0^{*}(-\mathbf{k})\,e^{i\omega t}, \quad \omega = \Big\lfloor \frac{\sqrt{g k \tanh(k d)}}{\omega_0} \Big\rfloor\,\omega_0, \quad \omega_0 = \frac{2\pi}{120\ \text{s}}, \quad e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta

					
d is the water depth the engine uses, 60 studs (the engine treats a stud as a metre), deep enough that tanh(kd) is close to 1 for all but the longest waves, so ω is close to √(gk) and long waves outrun short ones. ω₀ is one turn per 120 s, and the code rounds each ω down to a whole number of those (the ⌊ ⌋ brackets), so the sea repeats every 120 s. Euler's formula turns each wave into an arrow spinning in the complex plane.

				

			

		


		

			

				
The FFT

				
Adding up thousands of waves at every point, one by one, is hopeless. The fast Fourier transform does the same sum for a whole grid at once by reusing work; set the waves per side and this page times both ways, right now, in your browser.

				

				
				

					The math
					
h(\mathbf{x}, t) = \sum_{\mathbf{k}} \tilde h(\mathbf{k}, t)\,e^{i\,\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}, \qquad \underbrace{O(\htmlClass{t-n}{N}^4)}_{\text{wave by wave}} \;\to\; \underbrace{O(\htmlClass{t-n}{N}^2 \log \htmlClass{t-n}{N})}_{\text{FFT}}, \qquad \begin{aligned} X &= E + W^k O \\ Y &= E - W^k O \end{aligned}

					
For an N × N grid, summing wave by wave costs N⁴ steps. The radix-2 FFT splits the sum in half again and again, and each butterfly (X and Y) gets two answers out of one multiply.

				

				

					
Roblox says no

					

						
Normally

						
The FFT runs on the GPU as a compute shader, alongside everything else.

						
In Roblox

						
No GPU compute. The FFT is written in Luau and runs on the CPU, on three worker Actors (Parallel Luau), 64 × 64 per layer.

						
What I did

						
Kept every layer at 64 × 64, so the whole sea is at most 3 × 64 × 64 = 12,288 wave components on a CPU script. Acerola's GPU ocean runs about 4 million.

						
The number

						
One 64 × 64 inverse FFT takes 1.00 ms in Studio on my MacBook Air.

					

				

			

		


		

			

				
Choppiness

				
So far the FFT only moves points up and down, and the sea looks soft. Run another set of transforms over the same waves and you get a sideways push for every point, toward the crests, which is what sharpens them. It's the Gerstner idea again, done for the whole spectrum at once.

				

				

					The math
					
\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x}, t) = \sum_{\mathbf{k}} i\,\frac{\mathbf{k}}{\lvert\mathbf{k}\rvert}\,\tilde h(\mathbf{k}, t)\,e^{i\,\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}, \qquad \mathbf{x}' = \mathbf{x} + \htmlClass{t-chop}{c}\,\mathbf{D}

					
D is the sideways push and c, the choppiness, scales it. Tessendorf's notes write the push with −i; the code uses +i, which moves water toward the crests, the way a Gerstner wave does.

				

			

		


		

			

				
Three layers of waves

				
One 64 × 64 grid can't hold both the long swells and the small ripples, so there are three, tiling patches of 256, 64 and 16 studs. The two smaller layers pile detail on top of the big one, so the 256-stud pattern gets harder to spot: it's still there if you look for it. That's how 12,288 wave components pass for far more.

				

				

					The math
					
h(\mathbf{x}, t) = \sum_{j=1}^{3} h_j(\mathbf{x}, t), \qquad h_j \text{ keeps only } k_j^{\min} \le \lvert\mathbf{k}\rvert < k_j^{\max}, \qquad \htmlClass{t-layer}{L_j} = 256,\ 64,\ 16

					
Each layer carries only the wavelengths that suit its patch, so no wave is counted twice.

				

				

					
Roblox says no

					

						
Normally

						
Each layer is a GPU pass, and they all finish together every frame.

						
In Roblox

						
Three worker Actors that take turns: each layer got a fresh result only every third frame.

						
What I did

						
Fade from each layer's old result to its new one, counted in frames.

						
The number

						
Before the fade it looked like 20 fps to me: the game ran at about 44 fps, but the waves only moved 15 times a second.

					

				

			

		


		

			

				
Chapter five · Textures

				
The answer is a grid of numbers

				
Here's what the FFT actually hands back: not a surface, but grids of numbers, one number per little square, like the pixels of a picture. Graphics people call a grid like that a texture, and each square a texel. A texture can hold colours, but these hold heights, slopes and sideways pushes, a set for each layer. Here are three of them for the biggest layer, live in your browser, drawn as pictures: blue below zero, pale at zero, amber above.

				

				
				

					The math
					
h_{ij},\ \ \partial_x h_{ij},\ \ D_{x,ij}, \quad i, j = 0 \ldots N-1

					
The height h, the slope along x and the sideways push along x, one value per grid cell: column i, row j of an N × N grid.

				

			

		


		

			

				
Reading between the grid points

				
Reading a value out of a texture is called sampling it. A point the surface is built from (the next step shows them) doesn't have to sit exactly on the grid, and when it doesn't, it blends the four grid values around it, weighted by how close it is. That's bilinear sampling. The FFT's grid also repeats: its last column runs straight on into its first. So a point past the last column wraps round and blends with the first one, and the sea tiles without a seam. The dot swings back and forth across that edge, which is drawn as a dashed line.

				

				
				

					The math
					
h(u, v) = {(1-f_u)(1-f_v)\,h_{00}} + {f_u(1-f_v)\,h_{10}} + {(1-f_u)f_v\,h_{01}} + {f_u f_v\,h_{11}}, \quad \text{indices} \bmod N

					
fu and fv are how far the point sits between its grid neighbours; the indices wrap round at the grid's edge.

				

				

					
Roblox says no

					

						
Normally

						
The graphics card's texture hardware does this blend for free, for every vertex and every pixel, wrap-around included.

						
In Roblox

						
Roblox's renderer samples the images I hand it (more on those in a moment), but I can't write a shader, so nothing samples my height grids for the mesh's points.

						
What I did

						
Wrote the blend in Luau and ran it for every point of the water that a finer ring doesn't cover, on screen or off, for each layer that point's ring uses, every frame (the outermost ring every other frame).

					

				

			

		


		

			

				
The mesh

				
All those numbers end up on a mesh: rings of points centred where the camera looks, each ring twice as coarse as the one inside it, so the detail goes where you're looking. The rings snap to a fixed grid as the camera moves, so the waves don't swim (slide about as the points move under them). Here the sea is calmed right down and seen from above so the rings show: nested squares, the finest in the middle.

				

				

					The math
					
\Delta_r = 2^r\ \text{studs}, \qquad \mathbf{c}_r = 2\Delta_r\,\operatorname{round}\big(\mathbf{p} / 2\Delta_r\big)

					
Ring r has its points Δr studs apart, and its centre snaps to the nearest multiple of twice Δr under p, the point the camera looks at. Twice, so the edge of each ring lands on the next ring's points and the two keep meeting.

				

				

					
Roblox says no

					

						
Normally

						
You hand the graphics card a mesh once, and tessellation hardware adds triangles near the camera and drops them far away, on the fly.

						
In Roblox

						
There's no way to give the GPU your own moving mesh, and no tessellation. The surface has to be built by a script while the game runs, with EditableMesh, and whatever detail it has must already be in the mesh.

						
What I did

						
Rings of patches around the camera, each ring twice as coarse as the one inside it, snapping to a fixed world grid as the camera moves so nothing swims, plus a horizon that reaches the edge of the world.

						
The number

						
224 patches in 5 rings on the top tier. Points 2, 4, 8, 16 and 32 studs apart, ring by ring.

					

				

			

		


		

			

				
Painted maps

				
The grids so far move the mesh. The look comes from a second kind of texture: images Roblox wraps onto the mesh and samples itself, pixel by pixel, on the graphics card, with a blend much like the one in the step above. Three of them are shown here: the colour, a glow mask (how much each spot glows) and the ripples, a normal map, which keeps a direction in every pixel so the light sees bumps far smaller than the mesh's triangles. I can't work those out pixel by pixel in Roblox, so two worker threads paint them into images and hand them over a piece at a time. This page does the same. These are its real maps, live in your browser, as its painters leave them.

				

				
				

					The math
					
\text{rgb} = \tfrac{1}{2}(n_x,\ n_z,\ n_y) + \tfrac{1}{2}

					
Each ripple-map pixel stores the surface's direction as a colour: the normal's x, z and y, each squeezed from between minus one and one into the range a pixel can hold. (z before y because a normal map keeps 'straight up' in its third channel, blue.)

				

				

					
Roblox says no

					

						
Normally

						
A pixel shader works out the colour of every pixel on screen, every frame.

						
In Roblox

						
No pixel shaders. Colour has to be painted into an image and wrapped onto the mesh, and ripples come from a normal map. A game script can't swap a material's images either (only plugins can): every image has to be handed over the moment the material is created, and after that I can only rewrite its pixels. That's also the only way the normal map worked on a mesh built by code; setting it afterwards, as a plugin can, broke the shine.

						
What I did

						
Paint the colour, glow mask and ripple maps on two worker threads and each frame hand Roblox part of them: one band of the colour map, and the glow mask or the ripple map in turn.

						
The number

						
Measured before I added foam, back when the game handed Roblox one whole image a frame: 0.037 ms of script time in Studio. Now each frame carries a band of the colour map and the glow mask or the ripple map (and once a lap, the foam's roughness maps), and I haven't timed that on its own.

					

				

			

		


		

			

				
Chapter six · The look

				
Foam

				
Where a wave gets steep enough to fold, it breaks, and breaking water turns white. The math can spot exactly where the surface squeezes together; each frame adds foam there and old foam fades a little, so it trails off in streaks.

				

				

					The math
					
J = {(1 + c\,\partial_x D_x)(1 + c\,\partial_z D_z)} - {(c\,\partial_x D_z)^2}, \qquad f \leftarrow \operatorname{clamp}\!\big(\htmlClass{t-fade}{\delta}\,f + \beta\,{\max(0,\ \htmlClass{t-whitecap}{w} - J)},\ 0,\ 1\big)

					
J (the Jacobian) measures how much the sideways push (c times D, from the choppiness step) squeezes the surface, and drops below the whitecap level w where the water folds. (∂xDz and ∂zDx are equal, hence the square.) f is the foam: it grows there by β times how far J is under w, and everywhere it keeps a fraction δ of itself each step.

				

				

					
Roblox says no

					

						
Normally

						
Foam lives in a texture the GPU updates in place, every frame.

						
In Roblox

						
No pixel shaders again: foam has to be painted into the colour map like everything else.

						
What I did

						
Run the foam on a painter worker and paint it into the colour map a quarter at a time.

						
The number

						
A 256 × 256 foam field, painted into the 512 × 512 colour map.

					

				

			

		


		

			

				
Glow

				
The last touch is the one Sea of Thieves is famous for: wave tops glow blue-green when the sun shines through them. It's light scattering inside the water, strongest near the crests when you look toward the sun.

				

				

					The math
					
E = \htmlClass{t-glow}{\kappa}\,\max(0,\ -\mathbf{s}\cdot\mathbf{v})^{3}\,\big(\tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{2}\,\htmlClass{t-sun}{s_y}\big), \qquad \text{glow} = E \cdot M(h)

					
κ is the glow strength. v points from the water to your eye and s toward the sun, so E is largest looking into a low sun. M(h) is a mask from the wave's height above the average surface: crests glow, troughs don't.

				

				

					
Roblox says no

					

						
Normally

						
A lighting hook or a pixel shader adds the scattered light, pixel by pixel.

						
In Roblox

						
No lighting hooks. The only thing a script can turn is how strongly each material glows.

						
What I did

						
An emissive mask painted from the waves, plus one glow strength per patch, set on 232 separate materials on the top tier (224 patches plus 8 horizon pieces) every frame. Sea of Thieves' published mask measured wrong on my waves (it lit their sides, not their crests), so the mask is height above the average surface instead.

						
The number

						
Those 232 glow writes take 0.062 ms a frame in Studio.

That's all of it at once: layers of FFT waves on the CPU, foam, glow and painted maps, with a script moving every point. On the top tier that's three layers, 232 materials and 18,144 points. Scroll back up any time; every step still works.

Every time Roblox said no

				
    
					
  • No vertex shaders: a script moves every point, every frame (the farthest ring every other frame).
    • 
					
  • No pixel shaders for light: Roblox's own lighting does the highlight and the sky reflection.
    • 
					
  • No GPU compute: the FFT runs in Luau on three worker Actors.
    • 
					
  • Workers take turns: a frame-counted fade hides it.
    • 
					
  • No texture reads from my own code: the heights are blended in Luau, point by point.
    • 
					
  • No moving meshes on the GPU and no tessellation: rings of patches built with EditableMesh.
    • 
					
  • No pixel shaders: colour, glow and ripples are painted into images.
    • 
					
  • No texture swaps from game scripts: every image is handed over up front.
    • 
					
  • No lighting hooks: 232 materials on the top tier, each with its own glow.
    • 
				

			


			

				
Things we believed about Roblox that turned out false

				
Each of these was treated as fact until a test said otherwise.

				
    
					
  • EditableMesh is capped at 8 meshes per player. It's a shared memory budget; fixed-size meshes are charged what they really use.
    • 
					
  • A script can give a material an editable image by setting its ColorMapContent. Only plugins can do that; a game script has to use CreateSurfaceAppearanceAsync.
    • 
					
  • Normal maps don't work on meshes built by code. They do, if the map is passed in when the material is created.
    • 
					
  • Luau costs about 0.1 microseconds per arithmetic operation. It's about 0.0026 microseconds, roughly 40 times faster.
    • 
					
  • Texture coordinates stay precise far from the origin. At texture coordinates of 100,000 the texels tore apart, so the sea re-bases them by whole tiles.
    • 
					
  • Normal maps show at any distance. It depends on the graphics quality: at level 6 the ripples are gone by 170 studs.
    • 
					
  • A running Studio picks up a newly installed plugin. It doesn't; restart it.
