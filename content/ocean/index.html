Building an Ocean in Roblox

				
I built this in Roblox, and Roblox fought me the whole way. The sea on this page is a port of that ocean, running live in your browser.

				
Scroll to build it from nothing ↓

			

		


		

			

				
A flat white plane

				
Every game ocean starts out boring: a flat sheet of points joined into triangles. Everything after this is about moving those points.

				

				

					The math
					
\htmlClass{t-grid}{(x_i,\ z_j)} = (i\,\Delta,\ j\,\Delta), \qquad y_{ij} = 0

					
A grid of points Δ apart, every height zero. The wireframe shows the triangles between them.

				

				

					
Roblox says no

					

						
Normally

						
You hand the graphics card a mesh once, and it takes it from there.

						
In Roblox

						
There's no way to give the GPU your own moving mesh. The surface has to be built by a script while the game runs, with EditableMesh.

						
What I did

						
Built the sea out of mesh patches in rings around the camera, plus a horizon that reaches the edge of the world.

						
The number

						
224 patches in 5 rings.

					

				

			

		


		

			

				
One sine wave

				
Lift the points with a sine wave and the floor starts to roll. Three knobs make a wave: how tall, how long, how fast.

				

				

					The math
					
y = \htmlClass{t-amp}{A}\,\sin\!\big(\htmlClass{t-len}{k}\,x - \htmlClass{t-speed}{\omega}\,t\big), \qquad \htmlClass{t-len}{k} = \frac{2\pi}{\htmlClass{t-len}{\lambda}}

					
A is the height, λ the length from crest to crest, and the wave travels at ω/k.

				

				

					
Roblox says no

					

						
Normally

						
A vertex shader moves every point on the GPU, thousands at a time, almost for free.

						
In Roblox

						
No vertex shaders. A Luau script has to move every point itself.

						
What I did

						
Wrote every point from a script, every frame: 18,144 of them on the top tier (the outermost ring every other frame).

						
The number

						
About 3.5 ms a frame in Studio with Luau's native code generation, 7.7 ms without it. Roblox documents native code for servers, and I haven't measured a player's client yet.

					

				

			

		


		

			

				
Many sine waves

				
One wave looks like a machine. Stack up waves with their own directions, lengths and speeds, and it starts to look like water. The waves come from the Roblox ocean I started from, and the slider adds them tallest first.

				

				

					The math
					
y(\mathbf{x}, t) = \sum_{i=1}^{\htmlClass{t-count}{N}} A_i \sin(\mathbf{k}_i\cdot\mathbf{x} - \omega_i t + \varphi_i)

					
N waves, each with its own height, direction, length and starting phase. The last few barely show: they're small, and fitting them to the tile's grid stacked a few on top of each other.

				

				

					
Roblox says no

					

						
Normally

						
Each extra wave is a few more instructions in a shader, spread across the GPU's thousands of cores.

						
In Roblox

						
Each extra wave is another trip of a Luau loop over every point, on the CPU, inside the frame.

						
What I did

						
Kept the count down. Steps 4 and 5 sum the 16 tallest. In step 6 the camera flies up, where the small waves would blur, so the page keeps only the 4 tallest.

						
The number

						
The whole bank is too slow to sum at every point, every frame: it takes longer than the frame it has to fit in.

					

				

			

		


		

			

				
Light

				
Shape alone reads as grey mush; light makes it water. The sun brightens whatever faces it, and a highlight and a reflection of the sky do the rest.

				

				

					The math
					
\mathbf{n} = \frac{(-\partial_x y,\ 1,\ -\partial_z y)}{\lVert(-\partial_x y,\ 1,\ -\partial_z y)\rVert}, \quad L = \max(0,\ \mathbf{n}\cdot\htmlClass{t-sun}{\mathbf{s}}), \quad F = F_0 + (1 - F_0)(1 - \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{v})^5

					
n is the way the surface faces, s points at the sun and v at your eye. L is the plain sunlight, and F (Schlick's Fresnel term) is why water turns into a mirror when you look across it.

				

				

					
Roblox says no

					

						
Normally

						
A pixel shader works out the colour of every pixel on screen, every frame.

						
In Roblox

						
No pixel shaders. Colour has to be painted into an image and wrapped onto the mesh, and ripples come from a normal map. On a mesh built by code, the normal map only worked when I passed it in as the material was created. Setting it afterwards, the obvious way, broke the shine.

						
What I did

						
Paint the colour, glow mask and ripple maps on two worker threads and each frame hand Roblox part of them: one band of the colour map, and the glow mask or the ripple map in turn.

						
The number

						
Measured before I added foam, handing over one image a frame cost 0.037 ms of script time in Studio.

					

				

			

		


		

			

				
Pointy crests

				
Real waves have sharp tops and wide troughs, because the water moves in circles. Slide each point toward the nearest crest as well as up (a Gerstner wave) and the tops pinch into points; push too far and they fold over themselves.

				

				

					The math
					
\mathbf{x}' = \mathbf{x} + \htmlClass{t-chop}{c}\sum_i \hat{\mathbf{k}}_i\,A_i\cos\theta_i, \qquad y = \sum_i A_i \sin\theta_i, \qquad \theta_i = \mathbf{k}_i\cdot\mathbf{x} - \omega_i t + \varphi_i

					
Each point also moves sideways along each wave's direction k̂, by the choppiness c times that wave's height.

				

			

		


		

			

				
The repetition problem

				
Fly up and the trick falls apart. The page keeps only the 4 tallest (the small ones would blur at this distance), and like every wave on this page they fit a 256-stud tile exactly, so the whole sea is one tile copied over and over: hard to spot from the deck, wallpaper from up here.

				

				

					The math
					
y(\mathbf{x} + \htmlClass{t-tile}{L}\,\mathbf{e}_x) = y(\mathbf{x} + \htmlClass{t-tile}{L}\,\mathbf{e}_z) = y(\mathbf{x}) \quad\text{when every}\quad \mathbf{k}_i = \frac{2\pi}{\htmlClass{t-tile}{L}}\,(m_i,\ n_i)

					
Waves whose wavevectors sit on that grid all line up again after L studs. Here L is the 256-stud tile.

				

				

					
Roblox says no

					

						
Normally

						
Tessellation hardware adds triangles near the camera and drops them far away, on the fly.

						
In Roblox

						
No tessellation. Whatever detail the sea has must already be in the mesh.

						
What I did

						
Rings of patches around the camera, each ring twice as coarse as the one inside it, snapping to a fixed world grid as the camera moves so nothing swims.

						
The number

						
Points 2, 4, 8, 16 and 32 studs apart, ring by ring.

					

				

			

		


		

			

				
Real ocean data

				
Instead of picking waves by hand, ask oceanographers. The JONSWAP spectrum, fitted to North Sea measurements, gives the energy at each wave frequency for a wind speed and a fetch (how far the wind has blown over open water).

				

				
				

					The math
					
S(\omega) = \frac{\alpha g^2}{\omega^5}\exp\!\left[-\frac{5}{4}\left(\frac{\omega_p}{\omega}\right)^4\right]\gamma^{\,r}, \quad \omega_p = 22\left(\frac{g^2}{\htmlClass{t-wind}{U}\,\htmlClass{t-fetch}{F}}\right)^{1/3}, \quad \alpha = 0.076\left(\frac{\htmlClass{t-wind}{U}^2}{\htmlClass{t-fetch}{F}\,g}\right)^{0.22}

					
U is the wind speed and F the fetch; ω_p is where the energy peaks. γ = 3.3 sharpens the peak, and a spreading function D(θ) fans each wave's energy out around the wind direction.

				

			

		


		

			

				
A random ocean, moving

				
A real sea isn't a neat set of chosen waves, so each wave from the spectrum gets a random height from a bell curve and a random starting angle. The arrows are real waves from this sea, turning at the speeds the dispersion gives them (short waves turn fastest, though long waves travel fastest), looping every 120 s.

				

				
				

					The math
					
\tilde h_0(\mathbf{k}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\,(\xi_r + i\,\xi_i)\sqrt{S(\mathbf{k})\,\Delta k_x\,\Delta k_z}, \quad \tilde h(\mathbf{k}, t) = \tilde h_0(\mathbf{k})\,e^{-i\omega t} + \tilde h_0^{*}(-\mathbf{k})\,e^{i\omega t}, \quad \omega = \sqrt{g k \tanh(k d)}, \quad e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta

					
The ξ are random numbers from a bell curve, picked by the seed (the New sea button). In deep water ω is close to √(gk), so long waves outrun short ones, and Euler's formula turns each wave into an arrow spinning in the complex plane. The random numbers come from a documented generator rather than Roblox's own, so this is the same kind of sea as the game's, not the same waves.

				

			

		


		

			

				
The FFT

				
Adding up thousands of waves at every point, one by one, is hopeless. The fast Fourier transform does the same sum for a whole grid at once by reusing work; set the waves per side and this page times both ways, right now, in your browser.

				

				
				

					The math
					
h(\mathbf{x}, t) = \sum_{\mathbf{k}} \tilde h(\mathbf{k}, t)\,e^{i\,\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}, \qquad \underbrace{O(\htmlClass{t-n}{N}^4)}_{\text{wave by wave}} \;\to\; \underbrace{O(\htmlClass{t-n}{N}^2 \log \htmlClass{t-n}{N})}_{\text{FFT}}, \qquad \begin{aligned} X &= E + W^k O \\ Y &= E - W^k O \end{aligned}

					
For an N × N grid, summing wave by wave costs N⁴ steps. The radix-2 FFT splits the sum in half again and again, and each butterfly (X and Y) gets two answers out of one multiply.

				

				

					
Roblox says no

					

						
Normally

						
The FFT runs on the GPU as a compute shader, alongside everything else.

						
In Roblox

						
No GPU compute. The FFT is written in Luau and runs on the CPU, on three worker Actors (Parallel Luau), 64 × 64 per layer.

						
What I did

						
Kept every layer at 64 × 64, so the whole sea is at most 3 × 64 × 64 = 12,288 wave components on a CPU script. Acerola's GPU ocean runs about 4 million.

						
The number

						
One 64 × 64 inverse FFT takes 1.00 ms in Studio on my MacBook Air.

					

				

			

		


		

			

				
Three layers of waves

				
One 64 × 64 grid can't hold both the long swells and the small ripples, so there are three, tiling patches of 256, 64 and 16 studs. The two smaller layers pile detail on top of the big one, so your eye stops finding the 256-stud pattern: it's still there, just well hidden. That's how 12,288 wave components pass for far more.

				

				

					The math
					
h(\mathbf{x}, t) = \sum_{j=1}^{3} h_j(\mathbf{x}, t), \qquad h_j \text{ keeps only } k_j^{\min} \le \lvert\mathbf{k}\rvert < k_j^{\max}, \qquad \htmlClass{t-layer}{L_j} = 256,\ 64,\ 16

					
Each layer carries only the wavelengths that suit its patch, so no wave is counted twice.

				

				

					
Roblox says no

					

						
Normally

						
Each layer is a GPU pass, and they all finish together every frame.

						
In Roblox

						
Three worker Actors that take turns: each layer got a fresh result only every third frame.

						
What I did

						
Fade from each layer's old result to its new one, counted in frames.

						
The number

						
Before the fade it looked like 20 fps to me: the game ran at about 44 fps, but the waves only moved 15 times a second.

					

				

			

		


		

			

				
Foam

				
Where a wave gets steep enough to fold, it breaks, and breaking water turns white. The math can spot exactly where the surface squeezes together; each frame adds foam there and old foam fades a little, so it trails off in streaks.

				

				

					The math
					
J = (1 + \partial_x D_x)(1 + \partial_z D_z) - \partial_x D_z\,\partial_z D_x, \qquad f \leftarrow \operatorname{clamp}\!\big(\htmlClass{t-fade}{d}\,f + g\,\max(0,\ \htmlClass{t-whitecap}{w} - J),\ 0,\ 1\big)

					
J (the Jacobian) drops below the whitecap level w where the water folds. f is the foam: it grows there, and everywhere it keeps a fraction d of itself each step.

				

				

					
Roblox says no

					

						
Normally

						
Foam lives in a texture the GPU updates in place, every frame.

						
In Roblox

						
Game scripts can't swap a material's textures; only plugins can. Every image has to be handed over the moment the material is created, and after that I can only rewrite its pixels.

						
What I did

						
Run the foam on a painter worker and paint it into the colour map a quarter at a time.

						
The number

						
A 256 × 256 foam field, painted into the 512 × 512 colour map.

					

				

			

		


		

			

				
Glow

				
The last touch is the one Sea of Thieves is famous for: wave tops glow blue-green when the sun shines through them. It's light scattering inside the water, strongest near the crests when you look toward the sun.

				

				

					The math
					
E = \htmlClass{t-glow}{k}\,\max(0,\ -\mathbf{s}\cdot\mathbf{v})^{3}\,\big(\tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{2}\,\htmlClass{t-sun}{s_y}\big), \qquad \text{glow} = E \cdot M(h)

					
v points from the water to your eye and s toward the sun, so E is largest looking into a low sun. M(h) is a mask from the wave's height above the average surface: crests glow, troughs don't.

				

				

					
Roblox says no

					

						
Normally

						
A lighting hook or a pixel shader adds the scattered light, pixel by pixel.

						
In Roblox

						
No lighting hooks. The only thing a script can turn is how strongly each material glows.

						
What I did

						
An emissive mask painted from the waves, plus one glow strength per patch, set on 232 separate materials on the top tier (224 patches plus 8 horizon pieces) every frame. Sea of Thieves' published mask measured wrong on my waves (it lit their sides, not their crests), so the mask is height above the average surface instead.

						
The number

						
Those 232 glow writes take 0.062 ms a frame in Studio.

					

				

			

		


		

			

				
The whole thing

				
That's all of it at once: layers of FFT waves on the CPU, foam, glow and painted maps, with a script moving every point. On the top tier that's three layers, 232 materials and 18,144 points. Scroll back up any time; every step still works.

How it's running for you

				
Measured in your browser, right now.

				

				
Your screen looks like a phone's, so the page picked the lighter tier by rule: 6,480 points instead of 18,144, and two layers of waves instead of three. I haven't measured this ocean on a phone myself yet.

				
The live ocean isn't running in this browser, so there's nothing to measure.

				
For comparison, in a Roblox Studio playtest on my MacBook Air, on the top tier, the ocean just before I added foam held 60 fps: a 16.65 ms median frame with 2.81 ms of script time. That's with native code on, and I haven't measured a live Roblox client yet.

				
I matched this page's look to Studio captures over three rounds of lighting changes. My verdict: looks pretty good. It isn't pixel for pixel the same, and can't be: a browser doesn't light things the way Roblox does.

  • No moving meshes on the GPU: a script builds the surface with EditableMesh.

Things we believed about Roblox that turned out false

				
Each of these was treated as fact until a test said otherwise.

				
    
					
  • EditableMesh is capped at 8 meshes per player. It's a shared memory budget; fixed-size meshes are charged what they really use.
    • 
					
  • A script can give a material an editable image by setting its ColorMapContent. Only plugins can do that; a game script has to use CreateSurfaceAppearanceAsync.
    • 
					
  • Normal maps don't work on meshes built by code. They do, if the map is passed in when the material is created.
    • 
					
  • Luau costs about 0.1 microseconds per arithmetic operation. It's about 0.0026 microseconds, roughly 40 times faster.
    • 
					
  • Texture coordinates stay precise far from the origin. At texture coordinates of 100,000 the texels tore apart, so the sea re-bases them by whole tiles.
    • 
					
  • Normal maps show at any distance. It depends on the graphics quality: at level 6 the ripples are gone by 170 studs.
    • 
					
  • A running Studio picks up a newly installed plugin. It doesn't; restart it.
