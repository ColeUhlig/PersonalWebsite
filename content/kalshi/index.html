Working paper · October 2026

Containing the Optimizer: Leak-Resistant, Calibrated Multi-Agent Strategy Discovery for Short-Horizon Event Contracts

Cole Uhlig

Independent research

Research conducted April–June 2026 · Market data January–May 2026

Abstract

Language-model agents can now propose, implement and evaluate trading hypotheses far faster than a human researcher. Two problems limit what such a system can conclude. An agent rewarded for a good backtest is an optimizer, and it will exploit any path to future information that the evaluation leaves open. A search that finds nothing is uninformative unless its sensitivity is known. This paper describes augur, a four-role multi-agent system (director, associate, critic and implementer, built on Claude Opus and Sonnet) that searches for directional strategies in Kalshi's 15-minute Bitcoin event contracts, together with two mechanisms that address these problems. A differential point-in-time audit tests a feature for causality by its behaviour rather than its syntax: it destroys the future of the input in five ways at up to sixteen checkpoints and requires the past output to stay exactly equal. Injection–recovery calibration, adapted from transit and gravitational-wave searches, plants features of known discriminative power into real market data and measures the weakest edge the evaluation stage recovers. Under the exchange's actual fee schedule the detection floor is an AUC of 0.70; under the flat placeholder cost used for most of the loop's evaluations it is 0.85. Across 99 research directives, 740 hypotheses, 669 implemented strategies and 489 walk-forward evaluations, no strategy met the acceptance criteria fixed in advance. Several effects are present before fees: a favourite–longshot tilt of +0.51¢ per contract (t = 11.6), a residual underreaction to spot moves of about 1.0¢, and short-horizon momentum of +1.03¢. Each is smaller than the fee plus the spread. Because Kalshi rounds its fee up per order, the net value of the strongest effect depends on order size: it changes sign between one and five contracts per order.

Keywords: LLM agents; automated strategy discovery; look-ahead bias; metamorphic testing; injection–recovery; prediction markets; favourite–longshot bias; transaction costs.

1Introduction

Systems that use large language models to generate and test quantitative trading ideas are an active area of research [1], [2], [3]. In the typical design, a model proposes a factor or a rule, code is generated to compute it, and a backtest scores the result, which feeds the next round of proposals. Generating and implementing hypotheses has become cheap. The open problem is whether the evaluation can be trusted, and that is the subject of this paper.

The first difficulty is that the generator is an optimizer. Leakage, where a model uses information that would not be available at prediction time, is a well-known failure in data mining [4]. When humans write the features, leakage is accidental and rare. When a model writes thousands of features and is selected on backtest performance, every leakage path the harness leaves open attracts it. Frontier models have been observed exploiting weaknesses in the evaluations that score them [5]. The usual defence is a list of forbidden patterns (negative shifts, centred windows, whole-sample statistics), and it has a structural weakness: an open-ended code generator writes idioms that nobody listed.

The second difficulty is the uninterpretable null. The multiple-testing literature in finance controls false discoveries [6], [7], [8]. It says much less about false negatives. If an automated search reports that no strategy qualified, that report means little until one knows how strong an edge would have had to be for the search to find it.

The setting is Kalshi's 15-minute Bitcoin up/down contracts (series KXBTC15M). A contract settles every fifteen minutes, so there are 96 settlements a day, each with an unambiguous outcome. The exchange charges a fee that is convex in price and rounded up per order, which turns out to matter. This makes the series a useful laboratory: many independent trials, exact ground truth, and a cost structure that is easy to state and easy to get wrong.

This paper makes the following contributions:

  1. A containment architecture that treats the generating agents as untrusted (§4). Each agent call is stateless and has no tools. It runs from a working directory with no project files in it, and its only channel to the world is its text output. Settlement labels are never in scope for generated code. Every strategy outcome, including every failure, is recorded as a typed status. Prior LLM alpha-mining systems target equity cross-sections and treat leakage mainly as memorization by the model. augur instead targets binary event contracts and treats its code-writing agents as untrusted optimizers.
  2. A differential point-in-time audit (§5), a metamorphic test of temporal causality [9] designed as the admission gate for machine-written features. Applied retrospectively to the system's recorded output, it corrects both errors of the syntactic lint the loop actually ran: none of the 59 expressions the lint flagged was a leak, and the lint admitted ten strategies built on a leaking idiom that the audit catches. It was built in May 2026, independently of and before the publication of closely related detectors in July 2026 [10], [11]. It differs from them in its adversarial value perturbations, its exact equality and its design as an admission gate.
  3. Injection–recovery calibration of an end-to-end discovery pipeline (§6), following the method used to measure completeness in exoplanet transit surveys [12] and gravitational-wave searches [13]. Under the real fee the evaluation stage detects planted edges of AUC 0.70 and above; under the flat placeholder cost used for most of the loop's evaluations the floor is 0.85. Statistical significance is reached even at AUC 0.52, so the binding constraint is economic, and the cost model is part of the instrument. To my knowledge, no published LLM strategy-discovery system reports such a calibration.
  4. An empirical account of a short-horizon event market (§7). It reports effect sizes before and after fees for a favourite–longshot tilt, short-horizon momentum, underreaction to the underlying, and passive market making. It also measures a fill-model bias of 8.9¢ per trade and states a tape-proven fill criterion that removes it. To my knowledge, this is the first documented analysis of Kalshi's 15-minute crypto contracts, which the principal empirical study of the exchange excludes [14].
  5. An analysis of fee rounding (§7.4). Because Kalshi rounds the fee up per order, a single contract at extreme prices pays up to 14 times the nominal fee. As a result, the net sign of the strongest effect found depends on order size, not on the signal.

§8 describes the platform the system runs on: an event-driven engine with 5.24 billion order-book events, an accelerated backtester and a correlated-parlay pricer.

2Related work

This section places the work against four literatures. Table 1 summarizes the closest systems along the dimensions this paper addresses.

2.1Language-model agents for alpha research

Alpha-GPT [1] and QuantAgent [15] introduced interactive and self-improving language-model loops for generating formulaic alphas. AlphaAgent [2] adds regularization against alpha decay. Kou et al. [16] build a multi-agent pipeline that mines and combines alphas. R&D-Agent(Q) [3] splits factor research and model development into cooperating research and development agents. CogAlpha [17] evolves alpha code with language models. All of these target equity cross-sections. Where they address leakage at all, they mostly treat it as memorization: the risk that the model already knows the test period. R&D-Agent(Q), for instance, places the model's training cutoff before the test period and never shows it raw data, a concern also studied directly for GPT-based return prediction [18]. Leakage introduced by the code the agent writes is a different threat. CogAlpha includes a temporal-leakage check. Two systems published in July 2026 execute behavioural tests: Luo et al. [10] recompute LLM-written factors after perturbing every row past a cutoff and truncating at several endpoints, and XALPHA [11] applies truncation and future-noise perturbation tests. Open-source research tools contain related causality assertions [19]. The audit in §5 was implemented in May 2026, independently of these systems and before they were published. It differs in perturbing values adversarially (missing values, 10^{12}, permutation), in requiring exact equality rather than a numerical tolerance, and in its design as the admission gate to a sealed store. None of the systems above targets event contracts or reports a calibrated detection floor.

2.2Leakage and metamorphic testing

Kaufman et al. [4] formalized leakage in data mining, and Kapoor and Narayanan [20] documented its role in irreproducible machine-learning science. Metamorphic testing [21], [9] checks a program against relations between outputs on related inputs when the correct output is unknown. Equation (2) is such a relation, and the audit is a metamorphic test of temporal causality.

2.3Calibration by injection and recovery

Transit surveys measure pipeline completeness by injecting synthetic transits into real light curves [22], [12]. Gravitational-wave observatories inject signals into the detectors themselves [13]. In finance, synthetic markets with known ground truth have been used to compare out-of-sample testing methods [23]. To my knowledge, no published language-model strategy-discovery system reports an injection–recovery calibration of its evaluation stage under its real cost model.

2.4Prediction markets

Prediction markets aggregate information efficiently but not perfectly [24]. The favourite–longshot bias, where longshots are overpriced and favourites underpriced, is among the most robust regularities in betting markets [25], [26]. Bürgi, Deng and Whelan [14] study about 46,000 Kalshi contracts from 2021 to April 2025. They find a clear favourite–longshot bias and describe the fee schedule, but they explicitly exclude markets that reset every hour, such as cryptocurrencies and stock indices. Gebele and Matthes [27] attribute discounts on near-certain contracts to the cost of locking up capital. Young [28] finds that quotes in Polymarket's 15-minute Bitcoin markets respond to large Binance moves after a median of 347 ms. That market is the nearest analogue of the one studied here.

2.5Maximum-entropy models of correlated outcomes

The pairwise maximum-entropy distribution [29] is the least-structured model consistent with given means and pairwise correlations. Schneidman et al. [30] showed that it captures strongly correlated network states from weak pairwise correlations. Rana et al. [31] use a shared pairwise model of dependencies to make markets in parlay-style joint contracts, learning it from trading. §8.4 fits couplings offline from box scores for same-game legs.

3Market and data

3.1The contract

Each KXBTC15M contract refers to one fifteen-minute window. The YES side pays $1 if the CF Benchmarks Bitcoin Real-Time Index at the end of the window is above its value at the start, and nothing otherwise. Prices are quoted in whole cents from 1¢ to 99¢. I checked the settlement rule against an independent 1-second spot series: on decisive windows (a move of more than 20 basis points), labels derived from spot agree with the terminal state of the order book 97.1% of the time. The disagreements concentrate in near-ties, where the proxy exchange and the index differ.

3.2The fee

Kalshi charges a taker fee on each order of C contracts at price P (in dollars, so 0 < P < 1) of

F(C,P) = \big\lceil 7\, C\, P\,(1-P) \big\rceil \ \text{cents},
(1)

that is, 7% of the variance of a $1 binary payoff per contract, rounded up to the next cent for the whole order [32]. The fee peaks at P = 0.5 (1.75¢ per contract before rounding) and vanishes at the extremes. Rounding per order makes the fee per contract depend on order size (§7.4).

3.3Data

Order books and trades. Historical Kalshi data from Predexon were converted into the native catalog of an event-driven trading engine (§8): 5.24 billion level-2 order-book deltas (covering 9,991 contracts) and 64.6 million trades (21,670 contracts), from a registry of 28,006 instruments. The agent loop works on 5-second bars derived from this record. Spot. Binance BTCUSDT 1-second klines for 1 January to 5 May 2026 (10.8 million seconds), plus Coinbase 1-minute bars. The evaluation windows for each analysis are stated with its results. The data are licensed and are not redistributed.

4System architecture

4.1Roles

augur divides research into four model roles and a deterministic harness (Table 2). The roles are modelled on the division of labour in a research group. One agent decides where to look, one proposes, one rejects, and one implements, so that no single model both generates and judges its own ideas.

Table 2. Agent roles. Effort is the reasoning-effort setting passed to the model; the command-line interface does not expose sampling temperature.

ImplementerClaude SonnetLowOne accepted hypothesisA written plan and a Python strategy(data, feat)

The loop closes through the ledger. Before each directive the director reads the ledger of established findings and a digest of results (the ten best, the fifteen most recent and aggregate outcome counts), and its prompt rewards novelty explicitly: “Your job is NOT to write strategies. It is to decide WHERE to dig … rewarded for directions that are genuinely NEW TO THIS MARKET.” Hypotheses and code are hashed, so a repeated idea is detected rather than re-run. The critic acts as a cheap filter in front of expensive evaluation: “Be strict. The point is to not waste compute. Default to reject if you're unsure.” It accepted about 70% of the hypotheses it graded.

4.2Invocation as containment

Every agent call is a headless claude -p process launched with an empty tool list, no session persistence, a system prompt, and structured output. It runs from the system temporary directory, which contains no project files, and calls are throttled to 100 per minute. An agent cannot read data, previous code, the evaluation harness or its own past conversations. It sees exactly what the harness passes in and influences the system only through the text it returns. This isolation is the precondition for every later guardrail. An agent that could read the evaluation code could optimize against it.

4.3The implementation contract

The implementer writes one function, strategy(data, feat), against a small library of primitives that are point-in-time-safe by construction (rolling windows, positive shifts, true range, within-window quantiles). Before writing code it must produce a plan that includes a sanity check on how often the strategy will fire: “If your sanity check estimates >500 fires/day, stop and reconsider.” When the code fails, the harness returns a remediation specific to the failure rather than a bare error code. This was a deliberate change. Early runs showed that a bare lint code “told the coder LLM nothing it could act on — it re-emitted the same violation across all 3 retries.”

4.4Evaluation protocol

Strategies are evaluated walk-forward over four rolling windows: one week of training followed by three days of testing, advancing three days at a time. Fills occur no earlier than the bar after the signal. The engine enforces this as an invariant and raises an error if it is configured otherwise. Costs follow equation (1) from 27 May 2026. The large searches ran on 24–25 May with a flat placeholder of $0.10 per trade, and 472 of the 489 evaluations in §7 used it; §6 measures what that choice cost in sensitivity. A strategy qualifies only if it meets all four criteria, which were fixed before the searches were run: net profit of at least $0.15 per trade, at least 200 trades, at least 75% of test windows positive, and no test window worse than −$0.05 per trade. An implausibility check, added on 26 May, flags results that are too good to be credible (a hit rate above 0.90, an absolute net above $0.40 per trade, or every window positive with a net above $0.25) as suspected leaks for review.

4.5Fail-closed accounting

Every strategy attempt becomes a typed row in SQLite: success, lint rejection, runtime error, timeout, malformed output, missing code block, engine error, or unrecoverable. No attempt is dropped silently. If the critic call itself fails, all hypotheses are accepted and the failure is logged, so a failure costs compute but never narrows what is tested. The schema runs from directives to hypotheses to attempts to walk-forward runs, so any number in §7 can be traced back to the directive that produced it.

5Leakage guardrails

5.1Threat model

The adversary is the implementer. It is not malicious, but it is selected on outcomes, and that is enough. Four channels could inflate a backtest. (i) Label access: reading settlement outcomes directly. (ii) Temporal leakage: negative shifts, centred windows, statistics over the whole sample, or per-contract aggregates that include rows after the decision time. (iii) Evaluation escape: using dynamic evaluation, imports or attribute traversal to reach harness internals. (iv) Execution optimism: filling on the bar that generated the signal. Channel (i) is closed by never injecting labels into the sandbox namespace and (iv) by the fill-lag invariant; channel (iii) is narrowed, not closed, by the lint and sandbox. Channel (ii) is the hard one, because temporal leakage has no fixed syntax.

5.2Syntactic lint and sandbox

Before execution, a static pass walks the syntax tree and checks seven rules: negative shifts, label references, whole-series aggregates, 25 forbidden modules, dynamic eval, dynamic attribute access, and dunder traversal. The sandbox then compiles and executes the code with labels absent from scope, a hard timeout and a check on the return type. This layer is cheap and catches the obvious cases. As §5.4 shows, it is also both over- and under-inclusive for channel (ii).

5.3The differential point-in-time audit

Let a feature f map a bar sequence x_{0:T} to an output sequence y_{0:T}. The feature is point-in-time safe if its past cannot depend on its future:

\begin{gathered} \forall\, t,\ \forall\, x':\ \ x'_{0:t} = x_{0:t} \\ \Longrightarrow\ f(x')_{0:t} = f(x)_{0:t}. \end{gathered}
(2)

The audit tests this property by its consequences rather than by inspecting the code. It first runs f twice on the same input and rejects it as non-deterministic if the outputs differ, since a non-deterministic feature cannot be audited by comparison. It then chooses up to K = 16 checkpoints t_k: the dyadic positions 1, 2, 4, 8, … (because leaks often appear near the start of a series) and seeded random positions after that. At each checkpoint it builds a variant input x' that matches x up to t_k and is altered after it by one of five perturbations:

  • truncate: delete every row after t_k;
  • NaN-poison: replace future values with missing values;
  • random: replace future values with noise;
  • extreme: replace future values with 10^{12};
  • shuffle: permute the future rows.

If any output at or before t_k changes at all (under exact, missing-value-aware equality), the feature is rejected as a point-in-time leak, and the checkpoint and perturbation are reported. Each perturbation targets a different way of evading the others. Truncation alone misses code that pads or forward-fills. Missing values expose backward propagation of missingness. Extreme values expose normalization by a global statistic. Shuffling exposes reads that depend on the order of future rows but not on their distribution. The audit costs at most 5K = 80 perturbed evaluations plus the two determinism runs, and stops at the first violation.

The audit is one-sided by design. A rejection is a witness: two concrete inputs that agree on the past and produce different pasts, which is a constructive counterexample to (2). A pass is evidence, not proof. A leak confined between checkpoints, or one that is invariant to all five perturbations, could escape, and the test suite keeps such a case as a documented expected failure. In augur the audit was built as the admission gate for a sealed feature store, from which strategies would compose features rather than compute their own. In the runs reported here the store was not yet connected to the implementer, so strategies computed their own features under the lint alone, and the audit's performance on their code (§5.4) is measured retrospectively.

5.4Behavioural versus syntactic detection

The system's recorded output allows a direct comparison of the two approaches (Table 3). Across eight run databases the lint rejected 59 strategy attempts, every one under the whole-series-aggregate rule. I inspected the flagged expression in all 59, and every one is causal, although nine of the rejected attempts also contained, elsewhere, the leaking idiom described next. They are row-wise reductions across columns (max(axis=1) over open and close, for candle bodies, wicks and true range), prefix sums used as run identifiers, and statistics computed inside rolling-window callbacks, where the “whole series” is the window. Meanwhile, ten strategies that passed the lint computed a per-contract total bar count with groupby(...).transform("count") to measure time remaining to expiry. That quantity depends on bars that have not yet occurred.

On this corpus none of the lint's flags pointed at a leak, and it missed the one leaking idiom that recurred; where it did reject leaky code, it did so for the wrong reason. Each false flag cost a retry, and each miss admitted a leak into evaluation. Applied to the same idioms, the behavioural test made neither error. The admitted strategies happened to perform poorly, for example 60 trades at −11.7¢ per trade with no positive week, so no spurious finding reached the ledger. That was luck, not design. The comparison is the empirical case for moving leakage control from patterns to behaviour.

5.5Result-level gates

Two further checks act on outcomes rather than code. The implausibility check (§4.4) flags results that are too good to be credible. A later predictor-training stage raises an error, and fails closed, when cross-validated AUC exceeds a threshold; after a settlement model reached an AUC of 0.908, that model was re-specified as lift over a baseline that knows only the price (§7.2). That stage also reserves April 2026 as a lockbox with an irreversible reveal flag. The earlier loop runs and the calibration had already used April data, so the lockbox protects only work that came after it.

6Injection–recovery calibration

6.1Method

Astronomical surveys measure their completeness by injecting synthetic signals of known amplitude into real data and counting how many the pipeline recovers [12], [13]. augur applies the same procedure to strategy discovery. For each contract c with outcome y_c \in \{0,1\}, it plants a feature

\begin{gathered} z_c = y_c + \varepsilon_c,\qquad \varepsilon_c \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2), \\ \mathrm{AUC}(\sigma) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2}}\right), \end{gathered}
(3)

which follows from the equal-variance binormal model. Inverting (3) gives the \sigma for any target AUC. The feature takes one draw per contract and is constant within it, so it carries information about the outcome without being a timing leak. The planter measures the AUC it actually achieved and raises an error if that misses the target by more than 0.03, so a mis-calibrated plant can never be used silently. A fixed entry rule trades on the planted feature through the evaluation stage (the engine, the cost model and the strict criteria of §4.4); the agents, lint and sandbox are bypassed. The sweep used the engine's default protocol of four weeks of training and one week of testing, over 8 March to 22 April 2026. Significance comes from a permutation null that shuffles settlement values across contracts (n = 300), which keeps the dependence between bars within a contract intact.

Three conclusions follow from Table 4. First, statistical significance is not the bottleneck. Every planted strength from 0.55 upward reaches the smallest attainable p, and even 0.52 is significant at 5%. What screens out weak edges is the economic criterion. Second, the cost model is a first-order determinant of sensitivity. Under the real, price-dependent fee the detection floor is AUC 0.70. Under a flat $0.10 cost, roughly three to five times too high for this market, it would be 0.85, and any edge in between would have been invisible. Third, the floor turns the null result of §7 into an approximately bounded statement. Most loop evaluations ran under the flat cost, so the relevant floor for them is 0.85: within the hypothesis families explored, no strategy evaluated that way carried an edge the calibration would have detected at AUC 0.85. The bound is approximate because the loop used a shorter protocol (one week of training, three days of testing) than the calibration. The comparison in this table is what established that the placeholder cost was hiding edges between AUC 0.70 and 0.85, and the real schedule became the default the next day.

7Empirical results

7.1Search outcomes

Over its lifetime the loop produced the volumes in Table 5. No strategy met all four criteria. Iterations are fast: in one recorded iteration, the director issued a directive on volatility term structure near expiry, the associate wrote five hypotheses, the critic accepted all five, and the implementer's first attempt was rejected by the lint. Its second attempt, 33 seconds later, was sandboxed in 0.34 s and scored walk-forward on 1,979 contracts. The time from directive to verdict was 2 minutes 36 seconds, with no human involved. The marginal model cost was zero, since the Claude calls ran on a flat-rate subscription rather than metered API usage.

7.2Efficiency of the price

A gradient-boosted model trained on March 2026 to predict settlement from seven features reaches a cross-validated AUC of 0.908 over eight expanding folds. Nearly all of that is the price itself: in a feature-selection study on an earlier codebase, the price level accounted for 97.5% of cumulative importance. Across the cell scans, the in-sample upper envelope of edge after fees was about +5¢ for cells that traded at least ten times a day, and the few cells above +15¢ traded one to three times a day. Even before any correction for selection, the edge available after fees is bounded at about 5¢ per trade at useful trading frequencies.

7.4Fee rounding makes the sign depend on order size

Write the nominal fee as \kappa(P) = 7P(1-P) cents per contract. From (1), the fee per contract on an order of C contracts is

\begin{gathered} f(C,P) = \frac{\lceil \kappa(P)\, C \rceil}{C}, \\ \frac{f(1,P)}{\kappa(P)} = \frac{\lceil \kappa(P) \rceil}{\kappa(P)}. \end{gathered}
(4)

At P = 0.50, \kappa = 1.75¢, and rounding adds 14%. At P = 0.99, \kappa = 0.0693¢, and a single contract pays 1¢, which is 14.4 times the nominal fee. Any order of up to 14 contracts at that price pays the same 1¢ in total. The overhead is largest where \kappa is smallest, at extreme prices, which is exactly where the favourite–longshot effect lives. Table 7 shows the consequence for the 99¢ favourite out of sample.

The same signal is negative at one contract and statistically positive at five. The fee schedule, not the signal, sets the sign. This also suggests why the tilt persists: at the order sizes a small participant would naturally test, it does not pay. Two methodological points follow. A backtest at one contract overstates costs at extreme prices by up to fourteen times. A backtest at the nominal fee per contract understates them for small orders. This analysis values entries at the last trade rather than the executable ask and does not model depth. It establishes a statistical regularity and its fee arithmetic, not an executable return.

7.5Fill-model bias near fast moves

The underreaction effect allows a controlled comparison of fill models, holding the signal fixed (events of at least 8 bp, about seven a day, held to settlement). Filling at the recorded book quote three seconds after the event yields +5.0¢ per trade with 88% of weeks positive. Filling as a taker at the next print with a 1.5¢ penalty yields −0.8¢. Crediting a passive fill only when a later trade prints at or through the quoted level yields −3.9¢. The gap between the first and last models is 8.9¢ per trade on identical signals. The cause is measurable: about a third of the recorded “stale” quotes after fast spot seconds were never touched by any print within sixty seconds, so they were never actually available. A placebo that enters ten seconds later still “passes” under the quote model, which is the diagnostic. I therefore adopt a tape-proven fill criterion: a fill triggered by an event is credited only if a subsequent trade prints at or through its price. Even then, passive fills carry a winner's curse, because they are filled when the market moves against them.

8Supporting systems

8.1Event-driven platform

The data platform is a fork of NautilusTrader [33], an open-source event-driven trading engine, extended with a Kalshi historical-data adapter (5.24 billion level-2 deltas, 64.6 million trades and 28,006 contracts in the engine's native catalog) and a live Kalshi execution adapter, over 178 commits. The adapter normalizes timestamp units (seconds and milliseconds) and price units (dollars and cents) across sources. For backtests it synthesizes the settlement events the engine needs to close every position at expiry, inferring the outcome from the terminal trade price.

8.2Accelerated backtesting

For parameter search on an earlier strategy family, a tick-level backtester was rewritten as a struct-of-arrays Numba kernel. Amortized over a 45-combination grid on 200 sessions (751,510 ticks), it runs about 57 times faster than the Python reference with identical trade decisions and zero difference in profit and loss (one machine, single measurement). It supported 53,701 Optuna trials across ten studies, mostly with the TPE sampler and NSGA-II for multi-objective runs, all resuming from one storage file. Those studies tuned parameters in-sample and are cited for scale, not as evidence of an edge.

8.3Statistical controls

Candidates outside the agent loop faced Benjamini–Hochberg control of the false discovery rate [34], the deflated Sharpe ratio [7], and the probability of backtest overfitting by combinatorially symmetric cross-validation [8]. That last test uses S = 16 blocks, giving \binom{16}{8} = 12{,}870 train–test partitions. Model selection used combinatorial purged cross-validation with purging and embargo [35].

8.4Pricing correlated parlays

Kalshi also offers multi-leg combinations on sports outcomes. Legs from the same game are not independent, so multiplying their probabilities misprices the combination [31]. I model n legs as spins s_i \in \{-1,+1\} under a pairwise maximum-entropy (Ising) distribution [29], [30]:

\begin{gathered} \Pr(\mathbf{s}) = \frac{1}{Z}\exp\!\Big(\sum_i h_i s_i + \sum_{i<j} J_{ij}\, s_i s_j\Big), \\ J_{ij} = \tfrac14 \ln \frac{(n_{11}+a)(n_{00}+a)}{(n_{10}+a)(n_{01}+a)}, \end{gathered}
(5)

where n_{11}, n_{10}, n_{01}, n_{00} count joint outcomes across 25,771 historical player box scores (579 players), with smoothing a = \tfrac12, and h_i = \tfrac12 \operatorname{logit} p_i. The coupling is the exact two-spin log-odds ratio. The fair price of a parlay is \Pr(\mathbf{s} = \mathbf{1}). For up to four legs, Z is enumerated exactly over 2^n states. For five to ten legs, a naive mean-field solver iterates m_i = \tanh(h_i + \sum_j J_{ij} m_j) and bounds \ln Z with the Gibbs–Bogoliubov variational free energy. Two limitations are stated plainly. Setting h_i from marginals is exact only without coupling, and matching marginals exactly requires solving a convex moment-matching problem for h. Measured against exact enumeration, the mean-field approximation overestimates joint probabilities under positive coupling (by 7–27% for weak random couplings, and by a factor of 1.9 for a five-leg case with J = 0.3).

9Discussion

The scarce resource is trustworthy evaluation. In this system, a hypothesis cost nothing at the margin and a full implement-and-test cycle took minutes. Under those conditions the quality of the research is set almost entirely by the evaluator. If the evaluator can be fooled, the generator will find the way, and if its sensitivity is unknown, its silence carries no information. The two mechanisms here address those two failure modes directly. Both should generalize to any automated discovery system that writes code and is scored on data.

Behaviour, not syntax. A list of forbidden patterns encodes what its authors already know, while a code generator samples from an open space of idioms. On augur's own output, the list was wrong in both directions (Table 3). The differential audit asks the defining question of causality directly, and every rejection it makes carries a counterexample.

Calibrated nulls. Planting known edges converts “nothing qualified” into an approximate upper bound on effect size within the explored families. The calibration also exposed a dependency that is easy to miss: the detection floor moved from 0.85 to 0.70 when a placeholder cost was replaced with the exchange's actual schedule. A cost model is part of the instrument, not a detail of accounting, and the right time to calibrate is before the large searches, not after.

The market. At a fifteen-minute horizon, KXBTC15M is efficient to within its transaction costs. The effects that exist are the size the fee schedule allows to persist. The fee is convex in price and rounded per order, so its arithmetic varies sharply across the price range, and the most robust anomaly sits where rounding makes it uneconomic for small orders.

Limitations. The evidence comes from one contract series over about four months. Several effects rely on modelled rather than realized fills. Most loop evaluations used a placeholder cost, and the calibration used a different protocol from the loop. The sandbox executes in-process with the full set of built-ins, so it guards against accidental reach rather than forming a security boundary. The audit samples checkpoints and can miss a leak confined between them. Neither the sealed feature store nor a default-deny composition allowlist, both built and tested, was connected to the implementer in the runs reported, so the lint-versus-audit comparison is retrospective, not prospective. Agent behaviour depends on the model versions used.

10Conclusion

augur shows that a multi-agent language-model system can run the full research cycle on a live market without supervision: choosing directions, proposing and critiquing hypotheses, implementing them and evaluating them. Its distinguishing feature is that the evaluator is built on the assumption that the agents will exploit it. Agents are contained to a text channel. Machine-written features are tested for causality by their behaviour, with a counterexample for every rejection. The evaluation stage is calibrated by injection and recovery, so its silence has a measured meaning, and the calibration showed that the cost model is part of that meaning. Applied to Kalshi's 15-minute Bitcoin contracts, the system and the analyses around it found real effects, measured them before and after costs, and showed that the exchange's fee arithmetic, down to its per-order rounding, decides whether they are worth anything.
